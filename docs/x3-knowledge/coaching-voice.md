# X3 Coaching Voice — Reference & Line Bank

**Purpose.** This document exists so that any agent building the X3F TV app can write in-workout
coaching copy that sounds like it belongs to the X3 method, without copying X3 or Jaquish
Biomedical text. It has two halves:

1. **Research** (Sections 1–7) — what public sources actually show about how Dr. John Jaquish
   coaches: vocabulary, structure of a set, how he talks about failure, per-movement setup cues,
   and tone. Quotes are short, attributed, and used at most once per source.
2. **Line bank** (Sections 8–10) — 200+ **original** short coaching lines written for a 10-foot TV
   UI, organised by moment in the set and by movement family. Nothing in the line bank is copied
   from any source; it is original writing in the spirit of the method.

**Status:** researched 2026-09-07 from public web sources. Confidence markers are used throughout.

- `[CONFIRMED]` — stated directly in an official Jaquish Biomedical page, support article, or an
  official-site podcast transcript.
- `[REPORTED]` — stated by a credible third party (reviewer, long-form user account) but not seen
  in an official source.
- `[INFERRED]` — my reading of the pattern across sources. Not a fact. Do not ship as a factual claim.
- `[UNVERIFIED]` — could not confirm; flagged as an open question in Section 11.

## 2. The protocol the coaching has to respect

Coaching lines are only correct if they match the mechanics. These are the load-bearing facts.

- **One set per movement, taken to muscular exhaustion.** `[CONFIRMED]` Multiple sets are argued to
  be evidence of a weak stimulus, not a virtue.
- **15 reps minimum, 40 reps maximum, in full range.** `[CONFIRMED]` Below 15 the band is too heavy;
  at 40 clean full reps you move up a band. Partials are **not** counted toward the 40. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Tempo 2–3 seconds up, 2–3 seconds down.** `[CONFIRMED]` Slow enough that momentum is impossible.
- **Never lock out at the top. Never let the band go slack at the bottom.** `[CONFIRMED]` The
  rationale given is continuous occlusion/hypoxia and no neural rest.
- **Diminishing range.** When full range fails, you keep going with progressively shorter reps —
  strong range first, then mid, then the weak/stretched range. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **The set ends when the bar will not move.** Public restatements of the protocol describe
  continuing "until you can no longer move the bar even an inch" (Jaquish Biomedical support
  material). `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Rest between movements = long enough to catch your breath.** `[CONFIRMED]` No fixed clock. Big
  lower-body movements may need substantially longer. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **No warm-up set.** The first few reps of the working set serve that purpose. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Push/Pull alternating split.** Weeks 1–4: Push/Pull/Rest repeating (4 training days). Weeks 5–12:
  Push/Pull ×3 then one rest day (6 training days). `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Movement roster.** Chest press, tricep press, overhead press, front squat, split squat, deadlift,
  bent row, upright row, bicep curl, calf raise, pec crossover; hypertrophy program adds band
  laterals, overhead tricep extension, seated calf raise, shallow front squat, stiff-leg deadlift,
  seated wrist curl. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Typical push day order** reported by users: chest press → tricep press → overhead press → squat.
  **Pull day:** deadlift → bent row → bicep curl → calf raise. `[REPORTED]` (exfatloss). Treat the
  order as a reasonable default, not a canonical fact.
- **Set duration** roughly 30–90 seconds of continuous tension. `[REPORTED]` (StrongFirst).

**Product consequence:** the app's coaching clock is *not* a rep counter running to a target. It is a
three-phase arc — full reps → partials → the last inch — with an open upper bound. Copy must never
imply "you're nearly at the number, then you can stop."

## 3. Characteristic vocabulary

Words and constructions that recur across the transcripts. These are the raw material for an
X3-flavoured voice. (Word *choices*, not quoted sentences.)

**Nouns he reaches for**
`fatigue` · `exhaustion` · `stimulus` · `adaptation` · `range of motion` · `strong range` /
`mid range` / `weak range` · `variable resistance` · `tension` · `force` · `stabilization` ·
`lockout` · `partials` / `half reps` · `deficit` · `compliance`

**Verbs**
`fatigue` (transitive: *fatigue the strong range*) · `exhaust` · `diminish` (the range) · `recruit` ·
`load` / `overload` · `shorten` · `go` (as in *go until you can't*) · `control`

**Framings that repeat**
- Load is described as *where* it lands, not how much: strong range vs weak range.
- A set is described as a **sequence of ranges**, not a count of reps.
- Effort is described as **fatigue**, deliberately — in the Human Performance Outliers transcript he
  explains he prefers that word because "failure" reads as dangerous to non-lifters. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Volume is described as **evidence**: needing more sets is treated as proof the stimulus was weak.
- Time is described as an **eliminated excuse**: 10 minutes, so there is nothing to negotiate.

## 4. How he describes the collapse from full range into partials

This is the single most distinctive thing about X3 coaching, and the thing a generic fitness app
gets wrong. Across sources the description is consistent and mechanical rather than emotional:

1. **The full-range rep dies at the top, not the bottom.** Because the band is heaviest in the
   strongest (most contracted / most extended) position, the first thing you lose is the last few
   inches of the press or pull. `[CONFIRMED]` — the strong range fatigues first is stated repeatedly.
2. **He narrates the transition as a *decision that has already been made for you*.** In the Adonyx
   transcript the shift is phrased as something you notice, then obey — you can't get there anymore,
   so now you're doing half reps. It is not framed as an achievement or a permission slip.
3. **Ranges are stepped down, not abandoned.** The described sequence is strong → mid → weak, each
   with progressively fewer reps: a large number of full reps, then a handful of half reps, then a
   very small number in the shortest range. `[CONFIRMED]`
4. **The terminal rep is measured in inches.** He describes final reps that travel roughly an inch,
   and support material describes continuing until the bar will not move an inch. `[CONFIRMED]`
5. **The effective load falls as the range shortens** — which is the whole point. As you retreat into
   the weaker range you are also retreating into lighter resistance, so you can keep fatiguing muscle
   without loading the joint at the position where it is most vulnerable. `[CONFIRMED]`
6. **No pausing during the transition.** He explicitly warns that pausing between ranges lets blood
   back in and forfeits the occlusion effect. `[CONFIRMED]` The shift from full to partial should be
   seamless — the rep just gets shorter.

**Copy implications**

- Never say "switch to partials" as though it's a mode change the user selects. Say the range is
  *shrinking*, in present tense, as an observation.
- Never say "you've earned partials." Partials are not a reward, they're the next section of the same set.
- Never introduce a pause word — "reset", "regroup", "shake it out", "take a second" — inside a set.
- The correct emotional colour at the transition is **matter-of-fact continuation**, not celebration.

## 5. Failure vs stopping

A precise distinction runs through everything he says, and the app should encode it.

- **"Fatigue" is his preferred word over "failure."** `[CONFIRMED]` (Human Performance Outliers). The
  reasoning given is that "failure" sounds like injury risk to newcomers. Practical effect: he uses
  clinical language for a very aggressive endpoint.
- **Stopping is a decision; fatigue is an event.** The repeated rhetorical move is that most trainees
  *choose* to stop while the muscle can still move, and then call that a set. He treats the choice
  itself as the error, not the number.
- **The endpoint is defined by the bar, not by the person.** "The bar will not move" is an external,
  falsifiable criterion. This is an unusually good fit for an app, because it gives coaching a
  concrete thing to point at rather than a feeling to negotiate.
- **But there is a hard floor.** If you cannot do at least 15 controlled full reps, the band is too
  heavy and it becomes a safety problem — his own words are that you have no business under that
  load. `[CONFIRMED]` So "push harder" is not universally correct advice; sometimes the correct
  coaching is "that band is wrong."
- **And a hard ceiling.** He treats excess volume as damage, and damage as the enemy of growth.
  `[CONFIRMED]` (Peak Human). So the app must never encourage a second set, extra sets, or "one more
  round" — that is off-method.
- **Pain is not the target; fatigue is.** `[INFERRED]` from the consistent use of fatigue/exhaustion
  vocabulary and the joint-safety rationale for diminishing range. The sources do not give an explicit
  pain-vs-fatigue script, so treat any such line in the app as our own safety layer, not his.

**Product consequence — the safety carve-out.** Jaquish's public register is deliberately
uncompromising. A consumer TV app should keep his *directness* but must not inherit an
"ignore-everything" ethic. Recommended rule: the app pushes hard against **effort hesitation** and
never pushes against **joint pain, breath loss, or dizziness**. Section 10 includes explicit
off-ramp lines for that.

## 6. Per-movement setup cues that repeat

Confidence is lower here than elsewhere because the official form pages went behind a login. Each
line is marked. Treat `[REPORTED]` and `[INFERRED]` items as drafts to verify against the official
videos before shipping them as instructions.

### Universal, every movement
- Band goes **doubled over** for the heavy movements. `[CONFIRMED]`
- On the ground plate, stand so the band sits in the **middle of the band channel**, and your weight
  is centred over it. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Band runs **midfoot** for most standing movements; **balls of the feet** for calf raise. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Band path stays **straight up and down** through the rep. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Never release tension while standing on the plate. `[CONFIRMED]` (safety guidance in the quick-start material)
- No lockout at the top; no slack at the bottom. `[CONFIRMED]`
- 2–3 seconds each direction. `[CONFIRMED]`

### Chest press
- Band doubled over, then **threaded around the shoulders** — head and one shoulder through the gap
  between band and bar, band sitting **between the deltoid and the triceps**, then repeat on the
  other side. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Bar rests **on top of the shoulder muscles** at the start. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Press **forward and slightly downward**, elbows flaring, lats engaged. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Cue for pec activation: think about driving the **upper arm toward the midline** of the body.
  `[CONFIRMED]`
- Constant tension is what keeps the band from sliding — slack is what makes it slip. `[CONFIRMED]`

### Tricep press
- Same band-around-the-shoulders setup as the chest press. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Elbows stay in and the movement is an extension rather than a press away from the chest. `[INFERRED]`
- Band slipping is a known complaint and is addressed the same way as chest press — keep tension. `[CONFIRMED]`

### Overhead press
- Stand on the plate, band midfoot, bar starting at the shoulders. `[REPORTED]`
- The strong range is near full extension; that is where the band is heaviest and where you will
  fail first. `[CONFIRMED]` (general principle, applied here by inference)
- Stop short of lockout. `[CONFIRMED]` (general rule)
- Documented substitution: people with shoulder issues are directed to the **upright row** instead.
  `[CONFIRMED]`

### Upright row
- Stand on the plate, band midfoot, **narrow grip**. `[REPORTED]`
- Start standing tall, arms down, elbows slightly bent, shoulders rolled back. `[REPORTED]`
- Pull to **mid-chest only** — going higher is hard on the shoulder joint. `[REPORTED]`

### Bent row
- Same doubled-over band setup as chest press and deadlift. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Targets lats and biceps. `[CONFIRMED]`
- One user reported elbow tingling at one point in the range, fixed by not pulling all the way to the
  stomach. `[REPORTED]` — useful as a form-check prompt, not as an official cue.

### Bicep curl
- The prescribed version is a **drag curl**: the bar stays close to the body and is dragged up the
  front of it. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Elbows travel back**; hands do not drift out in front. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Stop at **mid-to-lower chest** — higher breaks bicep isolation. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Rationale given: shoulders going back plus forearms coming up makes a harder contraction at the
  top, and makes it a multi-joint movement. `[CONFIRMED]`

### Front squat
- Plate over the band with the band centred in the channel; band loops onto the **hooks on each side
  of the bar**. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Bar sits **on top of the shoulders**, across the shoulders and clavicle, lightly against the neck.
  `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Elbows forward and slightly up.** `[CONFIRMED]`
- Retain the bar with **thumbs or fingertips** only — it should not be held up by the arms. `[CONFIRMED]`
- It should rest on your shoulders and **not strain the arms or neck**. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Stand up slowly; keep the band tracking straight up and down. `[CONFIRMED]`
- There is a documented FAQ about **running out of breath during squats before the muscles are
  exhausted** — meaning breath, not leg fatigue, is the common limiter here. `[CONFIRMED]` (article exists;
  its exact remedy `[UNVERIFIED]`)

### Deadlift
- Band **doubled over and wrapped under the ground plate**. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Described as a hybrid between a conventional deadlift and a Romanian deadlift. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Shoulders pulled back**, then pull the bar straight up; band stays vertical throughout. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Do not lock out at the top — a locked-out deadlift dumps the tension. `[CONFIRMED]` (general rule;
  a user specifically reported feeling the difficulty vanish when he accidentally locked out `[REPORTED]`)

### Calf raise
- Band doubled over under the ground plate. `[CONFIRMED]`
- **Balls of the feet centred over the band channel**, body weight focused there. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Raise the heels; band centred and vertical. `[CONFIRMED]`
- Claimed to recruit and exhaust more calf tissue than weights can. `[CONFIRMED]` (a marketing claim,
  not an independently verified one — see Section 11)

### Split squat
- Listed in the standard roster. `[CONFIRMED]` Detailed cues `[UNVERIFIED]`.
- Third-party user reported split squats and deadlifts as the two movements that most reliably ended
  with him on the floor. `[REPORTED]`

## 7. The tone — what actually makes it distinctive

Seven traits, each with a note on how far the app should follow.

1. **Mechanistic, not motivational.** He explains *why the muscle is failing where it's failing*
   rather than telling you to want it more. The persuasion is anatomical. → **Follow fully.** This is
   the single most transferable trait and the cheapest way to sound like X3 rather than like a
   generic fitness app.
2. **Contrarian by default.** Conventional practice is the foil in nearly every paragraph. He is
   comfortable being the most disliked person in the room and says so. → **Follow lightly.** In-set
   coaching has no time to argue with an absent opponent. Save this for rest screens and session
   summaries, and never make the user the target of it.
3. **Blunt to the point of rudeness — about methods, not people.** The harsh words land on protocols
   and industries, not on the person training. → **Follow this boundary strictly.** Never write a line
   that insults the user.
4. **Certainty as a style.** No hedging, no "try to", no "if you can". Declarative sentences. →
   **Follow fully.** This is also just good 10-foot UI copy.
5. **Numbers used as anchors.** He reaches for concrete figures constantly — 15 to 40, two to three
   seconds, one inch, ten minutes. → **Follow fully.** Numbers are the most readable thing on a TV
   screen and they carry his voice.
6. **Economy framing.** Everything is justified by stimulus-per-minute. Ten minutes, one set, no
   warm-up, no cardio. The implicit promise is *there is nothing left to negotiate away*. →
   **Follow fully** at session start and session end.
7. **Dry, deadpan humour.** Occasional flat one-liners rather than jokes. → **Follow sparingly**, and
   only in rest / complete states.

**Anti-patterns — do not write these:**
- Hype exclamations, all-caps, "LET'S GO", emoji energy.
- "Almost there!" / "Just X more!" — the set has no known length. This is factually wrong here.
- "Great job!" mid-set. Praise inside a set is a rest cue and undermines the arc.
- "Rest 60 seconds." — the protocol says breath, not clock.
- "Add another set." / "Round 2." — off-method.
- Anything that implies the user should judge themselves. The bar is the judge.
- Anything that mocks the user, their weight, their prior training, or their pace.

## 8. Writing for the couch — the 3 / 6 / 12 word craft rules

A TV app is read at 6–10 feet, often peripherally, by someone whose eyes are watering. Three
length classes, each with a job.

### 3 words — the **glance**
Read in under half a second without turning the head. Used *during* movement, when the user cannot
focus properly.
- Grammar: bare imperative, or noun + state. No subject, no article, no conjunction.
- One idea only. Never two.
- Prefer words of one or two syllables.
- Good: `Slow the descent.` · `Range is shrinking.` · `Bar keeps moving.`
- Bad: `Try to keep the tempo controlled.` (too long, hedged, three ideas)

### 6 words — the **read**
Read in about one second, on a rest beat or a phase change. Room for one instruction plus one reason,
or two chained imperatives.
- Grammar: one clause, or two clauses joined by a comma. Never a semicolon.
- Put the actionable word first; the reason second.
- Good: `Shorter reps now. Keep them moving.` · `No lockout. Tension never leaves.`
- Bad: `You should be feeling this in your chest now.` (soft, no action)

### 12 words — the **linger**
Read while standing still: rest between movements, set complete, session complete, setup. Room for a
mechanism or a reframe.
- Grammar: one sentence with one comma, or two very short sentences.
- Earn the length with information the shorter lines could not carry — *why*, or *what changes next*.
- Good: `The strong range goes first. That is the band doing its job.`
- Bad: a 12-word line that is just an 6-word line with adjectives.

### Sequencing rules for the app
- Inside a set, **never show a line longer than 6 words**. Full reps and partials are 3-word territory
  almost exclusively.
- Change the line no more often than every 6–10 seconds inside a set. Flicker is unreadable and
  breaks tempo.
- One line on screen at a time. No stacks, no bullet lists during a set.
- Never animate text during a set. Motion in peripheral vision pulls the eye off the movement.
- Reserve 12-word lines for the three static moments: setup, rest, complete.
- Escalate length *downward* as the set intensifies: setup 12 → first reps 6 → mid 6 → approaching
  failure 3 → partials 3 → past failure 3 → complete 12.

## 12. Line-selection rules for the app

`[INFERRED]` — a suggested implementation contract.

- Tag every line with `moment`, `length_class` (3/6/12), `movement_family` (or `any`), and
  `intensity` (1–3). Escalate intensity as the set progresses; never escalate on the rest screen.
- **Never repeat a line within a session.** Repetition inside 10 minutes reads as a broken app.
- **Weight movement-family lines to setup and the first third of the set**, where form still changes
  the outcome. After the range starts shrinking, form cues are noise — switch to moment lines only.
- **Phase detection drives the copy, not the rep count.** If the app can detect rep-duration
  lengthening or range shortening, that is the trigger for the partials bank. If it cannot, use
  elapsed tension time with a conservative threshold and let the user advance manually.
- **The "past failure" bank should only ever be reached, never scheduled.** Do not show line 118 at a
  fixed 60-second mark.
- **Kill all copy for the last ~5 seconds of a set.** Let the screen be still while they finish.
- Localisation note: 3-word lines will not survive translation at the same length. Budget for a
  4-word ceiling in German and a 5-word ceiling in Spanish, or design the layout to reflow.

## 14. One-paragraph brief for a copywriter

Write like an engineer who lifts, not a trainer who shouts. Explain the machine, not the mood. Every
sentence is declarative and short. Use concrete numbers — fifteen, forty, two seconds, one inch, ten
minutes — because numbers are the voice. Inside a set, say only what the body must do in the next
three seconds, in three or four words. The set is a sequence of three ranges emptied in order, not a
countdown to a number, so never imply an end is near. When the full rep dies, do not celebrate and do
not permit a pause; just note that the range is shrinking and keep the bar moving. The set ends when
the bar stops, and the bar decides, not the person. Be hard on the method and never on the user.
