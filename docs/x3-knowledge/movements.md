# X3 Bar — Movement Reference

Durable knowledge base for the X3 training app. Compiled from public Jaquish Biomedical
sources: the official **X3 Workout Card** and **X3 Quick Start Guide** PDFs, the official
per-exercise form pages (`jaquishbiomedical.com/x3-program/exercise-form/*`, recovered from
the Internet Archive because the live pages now sit behind a customer login), the public
help centre, and product pages. Full URL list at the bottom.

**Epistemic status labels used throughout this document — respect them.**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[SOURCED]** | Stated explicitly in a cited Jaquish Biomedical source. Safe to ship as fact. |
| **[DERIVED]** | My reasoning from sourced facts (band routing, geometry, anchor point). Defensible but *not* a published claim. Do not present to users as Jaquish's words. |
| **[UNCONFIRMED]** | Could not verify from any source. Do not ship without checking. |

> **Read this before building anything:** two premises in the original project brief are
> **wrong**, and one is unverified. See [Corrections](#corrections-to-common-assumptions).

## Corrections to common assumptions

### 1. Calf Raise is a PULL-day movement only — not both days
The official X3 Workout Card (© 2021 Jaquish Biomedical) is a two-page card, one page per
day. Calf Raise appears **only** on the Pull Day page. It does not appear on the Push Day
page. The official site navigation groups it under "Pull Day Exercises". **[SOURCED]**

### 2. Upright Row is a PUSH-day movement, not a pull movement
Despite the name, X3 classifies the Upright Row as a **push-day deltoid movement** and
specifically as a *substitute for the Overhead Press* for people who cannot perform it.
Two independent official sources confirm this:
- The site's exercise-form navigation lists "Upright Row (Variation)" under **Push Day Exercises**.
- The help centre groups muscle targets as "Overhead Press and Upright Row: Deltoids."

The help centre is blunt that it is a downgrade, not an equal: it calls the upright row an
alternate movement and says it is inferior to the overhead press, because the overhead press
shortens the deltoid more. **[SOURCED]**

### 3. Tempo is 2–3 seconds up / 2–3 seconds down — NOT "1 second up, 4 seconds down"
I could find **no Jaquish Biomedical source anywhere** prescribing a 1-up/4-down tempo or any
asymmetric "slow negative" protocol. Every official source I found gives a **symmetric** tempo:

- **Quick Start Guide** (official PDF, rule 4): a rep speed of 2–3 seconds up and 2–3 seconds down produces the best results.
- **Tricep Press** form page: "Two to three seconds up. Two to three seconds down."
- **Overhead Press** form page: "2 to 3 seconds up, 2 to 3 seconds down."
- **Bicep Curl** form page: "Two to three seconds up, two or three seconds down."
- **Bent Row** form page: "two seconds up and two seconds down."
- **Split Squat** form page: "same cadence, two seconds up, two seconds down."

**Ship 2–3s up / 2–3s down.** **[SOURCED]**

The "1 up, 4 down" figure is a real thing in the wider strength-training world (it is a
common eccentric-emphasis prescription), but I found no evidence Jaquish uses it, and it
would in fact conflict with his stated logic — X3 deliberately loads the *concentric* strong
range hardest and *unloads* on the way back down, which is the opposite of an
eccentric-emphasis protocol. If a source for 1/4 exists it is likely a third party or an
older program revision. **[UNCONFIRMED — treat 1/4 as incorrect unless someone produces a citation]**

### 4. There are 11 movements with official form pages, not 10
Four core push, four core pull, and three push-day variations. Plus five more in the separate
Hypertrophy Program. Full roster below.

### Pull Day — core
8. **Deadlift**
9. **Bent Row**
10. **Bicep Curl**
11. **Calf Raise**

Raise page confirms calves come after the deadlift ("you've probably done the deadlift first
or another exercise that would exhaust your grip"). I recommend the site-nav order.

## Universal protocol (applies to every movement)

From the official Quick Start Guide and the form pages:

- **One set per exercise.** One set to exhaustion. Multiple sets are explicitly discouraged —
  the help centre's reasoning is that resting between sets lets the muscle recover and blunts
  the growth stimulus.
- **15–40 full-range reps**, then as many partial reps as possible, continuing until you can
  no longer move the bar even an inch.
- **Band progression:** start on the lightest band for everything. Move up only when you can
  complete 40 slow controlled reps with good form. Move down if you cannot complete 15.
- **Constant tension.** Never let the band go slack at the bottom. Never lock out at the top.
- **Tempo:** 2–3 seconds up, 2–3 seconds down.
- **Ground plate:** centre your weight over the middle of the band channel. Band aligns with
  **midfoot** for most exercises — **ball of the foot for calf raises** (the single documented exception).
- **Grip:** wrists straight, thumbs wrapped, no suicide grip — *except during the squat*,
  where the bar rests on the shoulders and the hands only guide it.
- **No warm-up needed** — the first few reps serve as the warm-up.
- **Never step off the plate or release the bar while the band is under tension.**
- **Band shortening:** wrap the band around the hook once or twice to take up slack. This
  *extends the weak range* and raises tension everywhere. Documented on the Chest Press page
  and in the help centre.

### Diminishing range (the defining X3 concept)
Once you can no longer complete the full range, you do **not** stop. You shorten the rep to
whatever range you can still move through, and keep shortening it until the last rep may only
be one inch. Because band tension falls as the band shortens, you are fatiguing the weak range
under *less* load — which is the safety argument for the whole system. Every form page repeats
this. **[SOURCED]**

### Strong range vs weak range (the reason this app needs per-movement strong-range data)
Jaquish's central claim: you are roughly **seven times stronger** in your strongest range than
your weakest, and conventional weights force you to load for the weakest point. X3's marketing
page states peak forces up to ~557 lb during full-range reps, decaying to ~300 lb in partials
and under 100 lb at final exhaustion. The bench-press example given is that mid-movement you
are at only ~25–33% of full capacity. Note these are **company claims**; BarBend's review
disputes that variable resistance is *superior* to free weights, citing research showing it is
comparably effective rather than better. **[SOURCED as claims, contested]**

## Band specifications (for the load model)

Official published ranges. Note the **singled vs doubled** distinction — doubling a band
roughly doubles the force at equivalent stretch, and *which movements are doubled is fixed by
the exercise*, not by user choice.

| Band | Singled | Doubled |
|---|---|---|
| Ultra Light | not published — "less than White at equivalent stretch" | not published |
| White | 10–50 lb | 100+ lb |
| Light Grey | 25–80 lb | 160+ lb |
| Dark Grey | 50–120 lb | 240+ lb |
| Black | 60–150 lb | 300+ lb |
| Elite | 110–600+ lb (spec does not clearly state singled vs doubled) | — |

The standard four-band set is marketed as 10–300+ lb across four tiers. Elite is the top of
the standard line; the guidance is to move to Elite when you can complete the full 15–40 rep
protocol with Black. **[SOURCED]**

A separate **X3 Performance / ParaForce** line exists (different rubber, "a steeper force
curve for greater peak output"). Exact per-band numbers for that line are not published.
**[SOURCED that it exists; specs UNCONFIRMED]**

### Doubled vs singled by movement — this is a hard constraint
The help centre names the doubled-over format as the one used for "the chest press, deadlift,
bent row, etc." Per-movement, from the form pages and Workout Card:

- **Doubled:** Chest Press, Tricep Press, Deadlift, Bent Row, Calf Raise **[SOURCED]**
- **Singled:** Bicep Curl — the form page is explicit: "single the band through the bottom. So not doubled, singled." **[SOURCED]**
- **Not specified in the sources I found:** Overhead Press, Front Squat, Split Squat, Pec Crossover, Upright Row. The Workout Card says only "the band is wrapped under the ground plate." For Split Squat the page says "a band hooked on each side of the bar," running under the foot near the heel. **[UNCONFIRMED — do not assert doubling for these five]**

This matters enormously: a White band doubled (100+ lb) outranks a Light Grey singled (25–80 lb).
Any band-recommendation feature must track routing, not just colour.

## 1. Chest Press

**Day:** Push (first exercise of the session)
**Alternate names:** X3 Chest Press; the banded analogue of a bench press / decline press
**Primary muscle:** Pectorals
**Type:** Multi-joint

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Hook the band on the bar and **double it over**.
- Grab the *band*, not the bar, keeping the band together, and make a triangle.
- Bring the bands over your shoulder **like a messenger bag**; grab the bar with both hands
  and rotate until the bar is cross-bodied. Drop the upper side down so the bands wrap
  underneath your deltoid. Drop the other arm through.
- The help centre's alternate wording: put your head and one shoulder through the opening
  between band and bar; the bar sits at ~45° in front of you; situate the band so it wraps
  around your arm **between the deltoid and tricep**; then match it on the other side.
- The band sits **around the shoulders, below the deltoids**. The bar rests on top of the shoulder muscles.
- **No ground plate.** The anchor is your own back/shoulders.

### Start position
Bar at chest, hands on the bar, band wrapped around the back and under both deltoids, elbows
bent and flared out away from the ribcage. **[SOURCED]**

### End position
Arms pressed **forward and slightly downward** — a decline-type press path — stopping **short
of lockout**. **[SOURCED]**

### Strongest range **[SOURCED]**
**At extension (the end of the press), but not lockout.** Jaquish: you deal with the heavier
weight when you are "at extension, not lockout but in a more extended position." The band is
longest and tension is highest there. As you return it is less weight, which protects the joint.

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- Focus on bringing the **upper arm toward the midline of the body from each side** — that is
  the pectoral action, and thinking about it builds the mind–muscle connection.
- Push **slightly downward**, mimicking a decline press — much easier on the shoulder joint.
- Keep elbows **back and flared out or slightly upward**, as far from the body as you can get
  them without the band sliding up your back.

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Resting at the bottom, letting slack into the band | This is called out as "one of the most common mistakes." Slack stops growth *and* lets the band slide around your back, leading to a failed set. Keep constant tension. |
| Feeling it in the triceps instead of the chest | Elbows are too tucked. Keep them back and flared out/upward, off the ribcage. |
| Elbows flared so wide the band rides up the back | Back off — flare as far as you can *without* the band migrating. |
| Locking out at the top | Never lock out; you lose tension. |
| Stopping at fatigue instead of continuing into partials | Shorten the rep and continue until you cannot move at all. |

### Band shortening note **[SOURCED]**
"Depending on your reach, you may want to shorten the band to extend the weak range of
movement" — wrap the band around the hook once or twice. Taller/longer-armed users generally
need this less; shorter users may want it.

### Tempo
2–3s up, 2–3s down (universal). The Chest Press page itself says only "slow pace." **[SOURCED]**

### Start / resting tension: **MEDIUM–HIGH** **[DERIVED]**
The band is anchored around your own back and pre-tensioned by the wrap itself before you
press. There is no slack position available — the setup is described as feeling tight/snug
just to get into. But the bar starts at the chest, close to the anchor, so the *stretch* is at
its minimum for the movement. Meaningful tension at rest, well short of peak.

### Load capability
High — see the ranking table. Doubled band, large multi-joint pressing musculature.

## 2. Tricep Press

**Day:** Push (second)
**Alternate names:** Jaquish notes weightlifters would call this a **skull crusher** — he says he dislikes the name. Sometimes "Tricep Extension."
**Primary muscle:** Triceps
**Type:** Single-joint (isolation)

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Band **doubled over**, wrapped around the shoulders **slightly below the position used in
  the Chest Press**. (Workout Card and the form page both say this.)
- Same messenger-bag entry as the chest press. The help centre notes the chest press and
  tricep press share the entry technique.
- **No ground plate.**

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Bar held **high — at forehead / eyebrow level**. Jaquish: "you start with the bar kind of
forehead level"; and separately, "instead of a chest level, you end up holding the bar kind of
at eyebrow level." Then **bend slightly forward, roughly a 45° angle**. Elbows tucked into the
sides of the body, upper arms **frozen**.

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Arms extended **downward and forward**, hinging **only at the elbow**, stopping short of
locking the elbow.

### Strongest range **[SOURCED, by extension of the universal band mechanics + the "push out" description]**
At elbow extension — the end of the press, short of lockout, where the band is most stretched.
The page does not use the phrase "strongest range" explicitly, but describes going "from here
to here" against rising band tension and applies the standard diminishing-range logic, which
presumes the strong range is at the extended end. **[SOURCED for mechanics; the explicit "strongest range = extension" phrasing is DERIVED]**

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Only the elbow moves.** "I'm not moving this joint at all. This is frozen."
- Keep elbows tucked into the sides.
- Stay exactly in that movement pattern — that is what isolates the triceps and exhausts
  nothing else.

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Moving the upper arm / shoulder (turning it into a press) | Freeze the upper arm. Hinge at the elbow only. |
| Holding the bar at chest level instead of eyebrow level | Start higher — eyebrow/forehead level, then hinge. |
| Standing bolt upright | Bend slightly forward, ~45°. |
| Letting slack into the band | Constant tension; no rest at the bottom. |
| Locking the elbow | Press out without locking. |

### Latex-allergy note **[SOURCED]**
Jaquish volunteers that he has a latex allergy and the band rubbing the upper arm irritates
his skin. His fixes: wear a long-sleeve shirt, or wipe the upper arm with a damp paper towel
to remove the latex powder. Worth surfacing in an app as an accessibility/comfort tip.

### Progression note **[SOURCED]**
"You will very quickly get stronger with this exercise, and you'll likely add more repetitions
with every workout, if not every other one." Good candidate for a fast-progression hint in the UI.

### Tempo
Explicitly "Two to three seconds up. Two to three seconds down." **[SOURCED]**

### Start / resting tension: **MEDIUM–HIGH** **[DERIVED]**
Same self-anchored shoulder wrap as the chest press, so it is pre-tensioned at setup. The bar
starts *high* (forehead), which for this band routing is the short/slack end of the path — the
band runs from the shoulders to the bar, and the bar is near the shoulders at the start. So:
pre-tensioned by the wrap, but near the minimum of its working stretch.

### Load capability
Low-to-moderate. Small isolated muscle, and the band is doubled but the working lever is short.

## 3. Overhead Press

**Day:** Push (third)
**Alternate names:** Shoulder Press, Banded Shoulder Press, Military Press
**Primary muscles:** Deltoids and trapezius
**Type:** Multi-joint

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Band runs **under the ground plate**, in the middle of the channel, aligned **midfoot**.
- Step onto the plate, **feet shoulder-width apart**.
- **Squat down to get the bar**, bring the bar up to shoulder height, then stand up. Now you
  are in position.
- **Hands pronated** (overhand) on the bar.
- (Doubled vs singled not specified in the sources I found. **[UNCONFIRMED]**)

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Bar at approximately **chin level**. The Workout Card: "Lower the bar to chin level."

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Bar pressed overhead to **just short of full extension**, with the shoulders pulled back as far
as possible so the head comes forward through the frame the arms make.

### Strongest range **[SOURCED — this one is stated very explicitly]**
**At the top, overhead, with shoulders rotated back.** Jaquish: "There's tremendous force at the
top of the exercise as Kyle's pressing. But much less stress on the lower portion of the movement
where the joint is compromised." And on the top position specifically — the arms come up forming
a rectangle and the lifter rotates the shoulders back to get the biggest deltoid squeeze, which
he calls **"head through the window."** He is emphatic: "This is not the top. This is the top…
You have to do this. This is very important. Because it's the true maximum contraction of the deltoid."

**For the app: "head through the window" is the single highest-value coaching cue in the entire
X3 library.** It distinguishes a rep that reaches the strong range from one that does not.

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **"Head through the window"** — arms form a rectangle overhead; rotate the shoulders back and
  bring the head through it.
- Keep the core engaged.
- Slow and controlled; do not speed up to chase a rep PR.

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Stopping at arms-straight without rotating the shoulders back | That is not the top. Push the head through the window for the true maximum deltoid contraction. |
| Locking out at the top | Stop just short of full extension. |
| Resting at the bottom | Constant tension; the muscle must never switch off. |
| Speeding up reps to break a personal record | Explicitly called out: "Don't do that… you break form; you get sloppy. That's when you're more likely to hurt yourselves." Better to do fewer reps and fully fatigue. |
| Can't hit 15 reps standing | **Regression: perform the overhead press from a kneeling position**, which reduces tension on all muscles involved. You will graduate to standing soon after. |

### Philosophy note **[SOURCED]**
"Your objective is exhausting the muscle. It's not getting one more repetition, necessarily."
A good anti-ego message for the app.

### Tempo
Explicitly "2 to 3 seconds up, 2 to 3 seconds down." **[SOURCED]**

### Start / resting tension: **HIGH** — highest of the whole library **[DERIVED, but strongly supported]**
The band is anchored under the plate at your feet and the bar starts at **chin level**, so a
large amount of band is already stretched before the first rep begins. There is no low-tension
start position available; you must squat down, pick up the bar under load, and stand up into
an already-tensioned position. The Quick Start Guide's warning never to step off the plate or
release the bar under tension applies most acutely here. This is why the Ultra Light band is
marketed for **overhead movements** as an entry point (see below) and why a kneeling regression
exists — both are start-tension mitigations.

Note the important asymmetry: **start tension is high, but it is still the weak range.** High
absolute load at the start ≠ strong range. The app must not conflate the two.

### Load capability
Moderate. Deltoids are smaller than pecs/back/legs, and the long band path from floor to
overhead means tension climbs steeply. The Ultra Light band's product page explicitly names
**overhead movements** as a case where a lighter resistance makes sense as an entry point.
**[SOURCED]**

## 4. Front Squat

**Day:** Push (fourth)
**Alternate names:** X3 Front Squat, banded front squat
**Primary muscles:** Abdominals, glutes, quadriceps
**Type:** Multi-joint

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Place the ground plate **over the top of the band**, band in the middle of the channel.
- Loop the bands around the hook on each side of the bar.
- Pull the bar under the top of your shoulders and **stand up slowly**.
- Bar sits **right on top of the shoulders**, slightly resting on the neck, balanced across
  shoulders and clavicle.
- **Elbows pointed forward and slightly up** — this is what keeps the bar balanced.
- Retain the bar with **thumbs or just fingertips**; you should not need much force.
- **Feet hip-to-shoulder-width, turned slightly outward.**
- Band should follow a **straight up-and-down path** (Workout Card).

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Standing upright, bar on shoulders, knees **not locked**.

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Bottom of the squat — hips dropped **straight down**, as if sitting into an imaginary chair.

### Strongest range **[DERIVED]**
**Standing / near the top of the squat**, where the band is most stretched. The form page does
not state this in the "strongest range" language used elsewhere, but the band is anchored at
the floor and lengthens as you stand, so tension peaks at the top. This matches the universal
X3 pattern of loading the extended end hardest.
**[The page is silent on this explicitly — flagged as inference.]**

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Drop the hips straight down**, like sitting in an imaginary chair. Practising with a chair
  behind you "is not the worst thing in the world" — let the glutes lightly touch, then come back up.
- **Knee-to-toe alignment:** look down at your kneecaps and try to see them in line from your
  eyes to your big toe. Knee and toe stay in line throughout.
- Do not lock the knees at the top — "when you lock your knees, you're resting and only loading
  the bone. Very little muscle activity happens."

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Needing your hands to hold the bar on | That means you are leaning too far forward or back and the bar could slip. Focus on balance and standing straight up. Elbows forward and slightly up. |
| Locking the knees at the top | Never lock; you lose tension and load the bone instead of the muscle. |
| Knees tracking inside/outside the toes | Use the eye→kneecap→big-toe line cue. |
| Running out of breath before the muscle fatigues | Documented in the help centre: thighs are large and demand a lot of blood flow, so the cardiovascular system can fail before the muscle does. |
| Grip | The Quick Start Guide's straight-wrist/thumbs-wrapped rule is explicitly waived for the squat. |

### Progression **[SOURCED]**
Front Squat → **Split Squat** from Week 5. Jaquish's hierarchy: split squat > front squat >>
back squat. His argument for front over back is ergonomic — nobody carries a heavy object
balanced behind their head.

### Tempo
Universal 2–3s. Not separately stated on this page. **[SOURCED via universal rule]**

### Start / resting tension: **MEDIUM** **[DERIVED]**
The band is anchored at the floor and the bar sits at shoulder height at the start — so a
substantial length of band is stretched standing still, comparable to the overhead press's
chin-level start but a bit lower. However you get *into* position by pulling the bar up and
standing slowly, and the start position is the top of the movement, so you begin at the
movement's own maximum stretch. Real resting load, less than overhead press.

### Load capability — **capped by the shoulders, not the legs** **[SOURCED]**
This is a critical modelling point. The X3 Squat Belt product page states the belt lets you
"double bands for more resistance than the standard bar-on-shoulders position allows." So the
front squat's ceiling is set by how much load your shoulders/clavicle will tolerate through the
bar, **not** by quad strength. Legs are the strongest muscle group but the front squat cannot
express that. The Squat Belt (routing load to the hips) and the Split Squat exist partly to
lift that cap.

## 5. Split Squat *(Push-day variation, Week 5+)*

**Day:** Push
**Alternate names:** X3 Split Squats, single-leg squat, Bulgarian-ish split squat
**Primary muscles:** One quadriceps + one glute at a time; heavy core stabilisation
**Type:** Multi-joint, unilateral

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Band hooked on **each side of the bar**.
- The band runs **underneath your foot and close to the heel** — note this is *not* the midfoot
  rule that applies to plate-based movements.
- Bring the bar up to the shoulders, **just like the regular squat**.
- Optionally use the **X3 Squat Belt**, which anchors at the hips instead of the shoulders,
  allowing doubled bands and a fuller range of motion.

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Standing in a split stance, bar on shoulders (or belt at hips), weight over the **front leg**.

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Descend until the **rear knee almost touches the ground**. The rear leg is there for balance
only — you should not feel much force through it.

### Strongest range **[DERIVED]**
Top / standing, same reasoning as the front squat — floor-anchored band, longest at lockout-adjacent standing.
**[Not stated on the page.]**

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Drive upward putting your body weight over the front leg.** All the force through the front
  leg; the rear leg is balance only.
- Rear knee almost touches the ground at the bottom.
- Expect heavy core involvement — Jaquish notes you will likely feel it **most in the core**
  afterwards, then the quads, because of stabilisation firing.
- 15–40 reps **per leg**.

### Why it is preferred **[SOURCED]**
Jaquish's argument: humans walk and run one leg at a time, so single-leg loading matches our
biomechanics. Training one side at a time concentrates "everything from your heart, your lungs,
all your resources" into one quad and glute. He also claims the extra stabilisation firing plus
loading raises circulating growth hormone, which he credits for X3 users' fat loss.
**[SOURCED as a claim — the GH/fat-loss causal claim is marketing, not established science. Do not present as fact.]**

### App-specific note **[SOURCED]**
The X3 **Force** bar (the force-sensing model) **defaults to Split Squats instead of Front
Squats**. The stated reason is purely instrumentation: during a front squat the shoulders push
against the bar hooks, producing "erratic and potentially wildly inaccurate force readouts."
The Squat Belt fixes this by keeping shoulders off the hooks. If this app ever integrates X3
Force data, front-squat force readings are unreliable by design.

### Tempo
Explicitly "two seconds up, two seconds down." **[SOURCED]**

### Start / resting tension: **MEDIUM** **[DERIVED]**
Same as front squat — floor/foot-anchored, bar starts high at the shoulders. With the Squat
Belt and doubled bands the absolute start tension is higher than the front squat.

### Load capability
**Highest of the lower-body movements** when used with the Squat Belt and doubled bands
(the belt exists specifically to allow this). Per-leg, though, so per-side band choice will be
lighter than a two-leg equivalent.

## 6. Pec Crossover *(Push-day variation, supplementary)*

**Day:** Push — performed **immediately after the Chest Press**
**Alternate names:** Pec Fly, Chest Crossover, Cable Crossover analogue
**Primary muscle:** Pectorals, in isolation
**Type:** Single-joint

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Bring the band **over your head and behind your back**.
- The band should **cross at the centre of your deltoid muscles**.
- **No ground plate.**

### Start / End **[SOURCED]**
The action is **pushing across the body, arm over arm, alternating one repetition to the next**.
Not simply pushing the arms out — you must genuinely **cross the body**.

### Strongest range **[SOURCED — stated unusually clearly for this movement]**
**The fully contracted, crossed position.** "You really want to feel the squeeze in the pectorals
in the fully contracted position. This is the most engaged your pectorals get." The whole reason
the movement exists as an add-on is that a bench-press-type movement never lets you reach the
pec's fully contracted position — so this movement is specifically for taking the pec to
"absolute fatigue in the strongest range."

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Align one elbow joint over the top of the other elbow joint** at the crossed position, then
  alternate which arm is on top each rep.
- Get the biggest squeeze you can in the pectorals at their shortest position.
- Do not just stick your arms out and stretch the band — truly cross the body.

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Arms go out sideways without crossing | Cross the body; stack one elbow over the other. |
| Same arm on top every rep | Alternate arm-over-arm each repetition. |
| Doing it fresh instead of after the chest press | It is designed to follow a pre-exhausting chest press. |

### Status **[SOURCED]**
Explicitly **"a bit of a supplementary movement. It is not required."** For people who want
serious pectoral thickness.

### Tempo
Universal 2–3s; not separately stated. **[SOURCED via universal rule]**

### Start / resting tension: **MEDIUM** **[DERIVED]**
Self-anchored around the back like the chest press, so pre-tensioned at setup, but the arms
start near the body rather than extended, and only one arm at a time crosses — so the peak
stretch is lower than the chest press.

### Load capability
Low. Isolation, alternating arms, performed pre-exhausted. Among the lightest-band movements.

## 7. Upright Row *(Push-day variation — substitution only)*

**Day:** Push (deltoids) — **substitute for the Overhead Press**
**Alternate names:** X3 Upright Row
**Primary muscle:** Deltoids
**Type:** Multi-joint

### Who it is for **[SOURCED — read this carefully]**
"Now, the upright row is **only, I repeat, only** for people who lack shoulder mobility that
cannot do the overhead press." Jaquish adds that biomechanically the upright row "is not the best
exercise for a whole host of reasons," but if that is the only mobility you have, that is what
you are left with. The help centre: it is an alternate movement and is **inferior** to the
overhead press, because the overhead press shortens the deltoid more.

**App implication:** never offer this as a peer option to the overhead press. Offer it as an
accessibility substitution, gated behind a shoulder-limitation flag.

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- **Same setup as the overhead press:** standing on the ground plate, band running **midfoot**.
- **Narrow-width grip** on the bar.
- Stand straight up, arms down, **elbows slightly bent**, **roll the shoulders back**, begin.

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Arms down, elbows slightly bent (not straight — constant tension), shoulders rolled back.

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Bar pulled up to **mid-chest — and no higher**.

### Strongest range **[DERIVED]**
Top of the pull (mid-chest), where the floor-anchored band is most stretched. But the range is
deliberately truncated for joint safety, so the movement never reaches the deltoid's true
strong range — which is precisely why it is called inferior.
**[The page does not use strong-range language; inference.]**

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Pulling the bar up to the chin** | Called out twice and emphatically: "You'll see people doing upright rows, or they get the bar right up at their chin, **don't do that**." It is very hard on the shoulder joint, and you are doing this movement *because* you are already compromised. **Mid-chest is the highest you go.** |
| Using a heavy band | "You want to start with a very light amount of resistance. So the lighter bands are preferred." |
| Straight arms at the bottom | Keep elbows slightly bent for constant tension. |

### Diminishing range **[SOURCED]**
When you can no longer reach mid-chest, the shortened reps "may end in your abdominal area"
until you can no longer move.

### Tempo
Universal 2–3s. **[SOURCED via universal rule]**

### Start / resting tension: **LOW–MEDIUM** **[DERIVED]**
Floor-anchored like the overhead press, but the bar **starts down at arms' length**, near the
anchor — the opposite of the overhead press's chin-level start. Only the slight elbow bend
maintains tension. This is one of the reasons it is the accessible substitution: it does not
force you to hold a heavily pre-tensioned position.

### Load capability
**Lowest or near-lowest of the library — by explicit instruction.** Lighter bands are preferred,
and the population using it has compromised shoulders.

## 8. Deadlift

**Day:** Pull (first)
**Alternate names:** X3 Deadlift. Officially described as "a mix between the traditional deadlift and the Romanian deadlift."
**Primary muscles:** Trapezius and hamstrings; glutes drive the movement; extensive whole-body stabilisation
**Type:** Multi-joint — Jaquish calls it "probably the most important exercise humans can do"

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- **Double the band** and lay it on the ground; place the **ground plate on top of it**, aligning
  the middle of the plate with the middle of the band.
- Band aligned with the **middle of the foot**.
- **Toes at the edge / front of the plate**, so your centre of gravity falls at the middle of the plate.
- **Double overhand, both hands pronated.**

### Grip rules — unusually strong opinions **[SOURCED]**
- **No straps.** "Your grip strength is a limit on what you can do in your deadlift. Leave it that
  way." Using straps gives "supernatural loading" on the back that your grip could not otherwise
  support. If you want a better grip, train without straps. (Exception acknowledged: an injured
  hand or inability to close the hand is "a completely different story.")
- **No switch/mixed grip** (he calls it the Olympic grip). Two reasons: with bands you can simply
  put the bar down when it gets heavy, so you do not need the drop-security a mixed grip buys;
  and a mixed grip puts the scapulae in two different positions, giving "uneven biomechanics."
  Both hands pronated gives equal loading on both scapulae.

### Start position **[SOURCED — and this is the key differentiator]**
**You are not pulling the bar from the ground. You are pulling from just below the knee.** At the
bottom position keep a little tension, and **reverse direction before you lose tension**. At no
point should there be slack in the band.

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Standing tall, hips extended, shoulders pulled back — **knees not locked**.

### Strongest range **[SOURCED]**
**Top / standing, hips extended.** The band is anchored at the floor and is longest standing.
Jaquish frames it via the injury argument: traditional deadlift injuries occur at the bottom
"where your back is most bent," and with variable resistance "you're using a lower weight in this
position," only fatiguing there after you have already fatigued the other ranges under higher force.

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Hinge at the hips and drive the bar up with the glutes** — Sarah "isn't pulling with her arms
  or really her lower back."
- **Back straight, not curved in either direction**, from the base of the neck to the pelvis.
- **Keep the trapezius engaged to control the shoulders** so they don't slump forward — "once they
  do, the back will start rounding. You do not want to do that. That is poor form, and that could
  cause an injury."
- Do not lock out the knees at the top.

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Rounding the back | Caused by the shoulders slumping forward. Keep traps engaged and shoulders controlled. |
| Trying to blast through the weak range with momentum (the barbell habit of bouncing off the floor) | "When you're using variable resistance, there's no momentum. Zero." Abrupt movements add risk. Better to pick a **lighter band and go slowly through that weaker range**. |
| Pulling with the arms or lower back | Hinge at the hips; glutes drive. |
| Letting the band go slack at the bottom | Reverse direction *before* you lose tension. |
| Using straps | Don't. Grip is meant to be the limiter. |
| Mixed/switch grip | Don't. Double overhand. |
| Quitting at full-rep failure instead of doing partials | Explicitly flagged as hardest here, because more total musculature is involved: "it's easy to just put the bar down at the end of that movement… see it through to the end because that's the part that counts the most." |

### Tempo
Universal 2–3s; not separately stated on this page. **[SOURCED via universal rule]**

### Start / resting tension: **LOW** — the lowest of the standing plate movements **[DERIVED]**
The bar starts **just below the knee**, close to the floor anchor, so very little band is
stretched at the start. Jaquish explicitly says to keep only "a little bit of tension" at the
bottom. This is genuinely the nearest-to-slack start in the library — the brief's intuition on
this point is correct. Contrast with the overhead press, which starts at chin height fully loaded.

### Load capability — **the heaviest movement in the system** **[DERIVED, well supported]**
Doubled band, the largest muscle mass in the body (glutes/hamstrings/back), a floor-anchored path
that lets you stand into maximum stretch, and no shoulder-comfort cap like the front squat has.
The only stated limiter is **grip strength**, which Jaquish deliberately preserves as the limiter.

## 9. Bent Row

**Day:** Pull (second)
**Alternate names:** Bent-Over Row, X3 Bent Row
**Primary muscle:** Latissimus dorsi (plus posterior deltoid, some biceps)
**Type:** Multi-joint

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Band **doubled** underneath the ground plate, **in line with the midfoot**.
- **Hands supinated** — the page says "supinated, which means pointing outward."
  ⚠️ **Source ambiguity:** "supinated" normally means palms *up/toward you* (underhand), which is
  the standard underhand-row grip and is plausible. But the page glosses it as "pointing outward,"
  and the *bicep curl* page uses the same phrase for a curl grip ("supinated grip, which means
  hands pointed outward or upward"). The Workout Card photos are not resolvable from text.
  **[FLAGGED CONFLICT — verify against the official video before shipping a grip instruction. Do not state this confidently.]**

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Bent over with a **neutral spine** — "not overly arched, not hunched, but flat back. So if
somebody looks at you from the side, your back looks like a straight line at an angle." Arms extended.

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Bar pulled **up and into the lower abdomen** (Workout Card).

### Strongest range — **THE MOST IMPORTANT EXCEPTION IN THE ENTIRE LIBRARY** **[SOURCED, emphatically]**
**The MIDDLE of the movement — not the top.**

This is the one movement where X3's usual "strong range is at the end" rule does not hold, and
Jaquish devotes most of the page to it:

> "With a rowing-type movement, your power position is not at the top, as you would suspect. It's
> in the middle. And it's because the biceps and the latissimus dorsi have curves that kind of
> cross each other."

He says the bent row is unique, that the mid-range "is actually the strongest," and that there is
a whole chapter on this subject in his book *Weightlifting Is a Waste of Time*.

### The consequence for rep counting **[SOURCED — build this into the app]**
Because the strong range sits in the middle, you will do **far more partial reps on this movement
than any other**:

- Normal X3 movement: 15–40 full reps, then roughly **4–5 partials** (he also says "two or three" elsewhere on the page).
- **Bent row: 15–40 full reps, then another 10–15 partial reps** in the mid-range, "because you
  still have so much power left in that position."

Direct quote: you "will go to complete fatigue with many more partial repetitions over all other
movements in the X3 program."

**Any rep-tracking or set-completion UI must special-case the bent row.** A user who stops after
4–5 partials here has under-trained the movement by design.

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Neutral spine** — flat back, straight line at an angle when viewed from the side.
- **Focus on feeling it in your back**, not the arms.
- Slow and controlled — "two seconds up and two seconds down."

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Hunching / rounding the back | Flat back, neutral spine. |
| Over-arching | Also wrong — neutral means neither. |
| Turning it into a bicep exercise | There is some bicep work but "not nearly the bicep work involved with the actual X3 bicep curl." Focus on the back. |
| Stopping after a normal number of partials | Wrong for this movement. Expect 10–15 mid-range partials. |

### Tempo
Explicitly "two seconds up and two seconds down." **[SOURCED]**

### Start / resting tension: **LOW–MEDIUM** **[DERIVED]**
Bent-over position with arms extended means the bar hangs low, near the plate anchor — little
band stretched at the start. Slightly more than the deadlift because the torso is pitched forward
and the arms hang from a bent-over posture, but this is a low-start-tension movement.

### Load capability
**High — second only to the deadlift.** Doubled band, large back musculature, multi-joint. Note
though that the working strong range is mid-movement, so peak band stretch is *not* where you are
strongest — meaning the top-end band tension is less usable here than in a deadlift.

## 10. Bicep Curl

**Day:** Pull (third)
**Alternate names:** **Drag Curl** — this is the specific variant X3 prescribes. Jaquish: "the
bicep curl that we recommend, and really this is the only way to do any bicep curl, is called the drag curl."
**Primary muscle:** Biceps
**Type:** The page calls it "a multi-joint movement" (because the shoulders travel back), though it is conventionally an isolation lift

### Setup **[SOURCED]**
- Place the ground plate on the ground and **single the band through the bottom — "So not doubled,
  singled."** ⚠️ **This is the only movement in the core library explicitly singled.** Do not
  double the band here.
- **Supinated grip** — "hands pointed outward or upward."
- Stand up straight, **elbows slightly bent** (never straight — constant tension).

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Standing, arms down but with a **slight bend at the elbow** so a partial contraction maintains
tension. "The bottom is just not with your arms perfectly straight."

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Bar at **mid-to-lower chest** — and no higher. "That's where the biceps are fully contracted. You
don't want to go any higher than this because if you do, you break the isolation of the biceps,
and you bring other muscles into play."

### Strongest range **[SOURCED]**
**The top of the drag curl, at mid/lower chest, where the bicep is fully contracted.** The reason
for the drag-curl mechanics is precisely to put load there: "at the top position, you have a very
hard contraction and a lot of resistance into the bicep at the top." He notes that in these
stronger ranges the load is "much higher than you would ever be able to use with the weights
because it's variable."

### Why a drag curl **[SOURCED]**
In a standard curl, at the mid-position "the centre of gravity is close to the elbow joint, and
you're actually not loading the bicep very much at all right here" — the lever arm dominates and
the strength curve goes backwards relative to your biomechanics. Keeping the bar close to the body
is "how you would normally pick up anything heavy."

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- **Drag the bar up the front of your body**, keeping it close.
- **Elbows go back. Your hands don't go out in front of you.**
- The bar follows a **straight up-and-down path**.
- **Squeeze the biceps hard at the top** — builds the mind–muscle connection.
- Help-centre phrasing: drag curls entail "both shoulders going back and forearms coming up,"
  producing a more intense contraction at the top.

### Common form errors → fixes **[SOURCED]**
| Error | Fix |
|---|---|
| Doing a traditional curl with the bar swinging out front | "Don't do this like a traditional curl. This is a drag curl." Elbows back, bar close. |
| Curling above mid/lower chest | Breaks bicep isolation and recruits other muscles. Stop at mid-to-lower chest. |
| Locking arms straight at the bottom | Keep a slight bend — constant tension. (He notes there is no way to lock out at the top with biceps anyway.) |
| Doubling the band | Wrong for this movement — single it. |

### Tempo
Explicitly "Two to three seconds up, two or three seconds down." **[SOURCED]**

### Start / resting tension: **LOW** **[DERIVED]**
Singled band (roughly half the force of a doubled band at the same stretch), bar hanging at
arms' length near the floor anchor, and only a slight elbow bend holding tension. Along with the
deadlift, one of the two lowest-start-tension movements — but for a different reason: the deadlift
is low because of *geometry*, the curl is low because of *band routing*.

### Load capability
Low. Singled band by prescription, small muscle, short range. One of the lightest movements —
though note the singled band means the *band colour* may look heavier than the load implies.

## 11. Calf Raise

**Day:** Pull (fourth / last)
**Alternate names:** X3 Calf Raise, standing calf raise
**Primary muscles:** Calves (help centre also lists hamstrings)
**Type:** Single-joint

### Setup — the one movement that breaks the midfoot rule **[SOURCED]**
- **Double the band**, lay it on the ground, place the ground plate over the top, then hook the
  bar to the band.
- **The ball of the foot is centred directly over the band channel** — explicitly **not** midfoot.
  "You do not want the band to be midfoot, like some of the other movements."
- Band aligned near the middle of the band channel. (If a smaller band splits, "just have it near there.")
- **Heels hang off the back of the ground plate** and **must not touch the plate during the movement.**
- Expect to feel slightly **pitched forward** — your weight is on the balls of your feet. You may
  notice this in a mirror or on video; it is correct.

### Start position **[SOURCED]**
Standing on the plate, balls of feet on the channel, heels hanging off the back, **heels lowered
but still under tension** (no relaxing at the bottom).

### End position **[SOURCED]**
Heels raised — peak calf contraction.

### Strongest range **[DERIVED]**
Top of the raise, heels highest, where the floor-anchored band is most stretched. The page
describes fatigue as the point where "Alex can no longer reach the peak point of contraction,"
which implies the peak contraction is the demanding end.
**[Not stated in explicit strong-range language.]**

### Coaching cues **[SOURCED]**
- Balls of the feet over the channel; heels off the back of the plate.
- Do not let the heels touch the plate.
- **Do not relax at the bottom** — constant tension like every other movement.
- Higher repetitions, slow and controlled.

### Band guidance — explicitly lighter **[SOURCED]**
Two stated reasons to use a **lighter band** here:
1. "The body responds to lower weight and higher repetitions" with calves, since calves contract
   all day long — he says this piece of traditional bodybuilding wisdom is actually correct.
2. **Grip fatigue:** "you've probably done the deadlift first or another exercise that would
   exhaust your grip. So it's much better to do it with a lighter band and higher repetitions."

This second reason is a scheduling artefact — the calf raise is last on pull day, after the
deadlift has already destroyed your grip. Worth surfacing in the app.

### Myth note **[SOURCED]**
Jaquish directly rejects the "you have to be born with good calves" bodybuilding myth, saying he
believed it himself and did not have calves before.

### Tempo
Universal 2–3s, slow and controlled. Not separately stated. **[SOURCED via universal rule]**

### Start / resting tension: **MEDIUM–HIGH** **[DERIVED]**
Counter-intuitive but important: the band is doubled and anchored under the plate, and the bar is
held at **arms' length while standing upright**, which means the band is stretched roughly your
full arm-hang-to-floor distance before you move — considerably more than the deadlift's
below-the-knee start. The *movement range* is tiny (a few inches of heel travel), so nearly all of
the tension is present at rest and very little is added by the rep itself. **This is the movement
with the highest ratio of start tension to working tension in the library.**

### Load capability
Low in practice, despite the calves being strong, because the limiter is **grip** (the bar is held
at arms' length, after deadlifts) rather than calf strength. Official guidance is a lighter band.

# Cross-movement analysis

## Load-capability ranking (heaviest-capable → lightest-capable)

⚠️ **[DERIVED]** — Jaquish Biomedical does **not** publish a per-exercise band chart. The official
instruction is deliberately individual: start everyone on the lightest band for everything and
progress per-exercise on the 15/40 rep rule. This ranking is my synthesis from (a) muscle mass and
joint leverage, (b) doubled vs singled routing where documented, (c) the explicit official
statements that certain movements should use lighter bands, and (d) documented structural caps.
**Anchors that ARE sourced are marked.** Use this to seed defaults, not to override user progression.

| # | Movement | Routing | Why it lands here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Deadlift** | Doubled | Largest muscle mass, floor-anchored full-stand path, no structural cap except grip (which Jaquish keeps deliberately). |
| 2 | **Split Squat (with Squat Belt)** | Doubled | Belt exists specifically so you can "double bands for more resistance than the standard bar-on-shoulders position allows" **[SOURCED]**. Per-leg, but the strongest lower-body expression. |
| 3 | **Bent Row** | Doubled | Large back musculature, multi-joint. Ranks below deadlift because the strong range is mid-movement, so peak band stretch is less usable. |
| 4 | **Front Squat** | Not specified | Legs are strong but load is **capped by the shoulders/bar position**, not the quads **[SOURCED via Squat Belt page]**. |
| 5 | **Chest Press** | Doubled | Large pressing musculature; capped by how much band pressure the shoulders/back tolerate before the band migrates. |
| 6 | **Overhead Press** | Not specified | Smaller deltoids + long floor-to-overhead band path = steep tension climb. Ultra Light band is officially pitched at **overhead movements** as an entry point **[SOURCED]**. |
| 7 | **Tricep Press** | Doubled | Isolation, small muscle, short lever — but doubled routing keeps it above the singled movements. |
| 8 | **Calf Raise** | Doubled | Calves are strong, but the limiter is **grip after deadlifts**, and official guidance is explicitly a **lighter band** **[SOURCED]**. |
| 9 | **Bicep Curl** | **Singled** | Explicitly singled **[SOURCED]** — roughly half the force of doubled at equal stretch. Small muscle, short range. |
| 10 | **Pec Crossover** | Not specified | Isolation, alternating single-arm, performed pre-exhausted after the chest press. |
| 11 | **Upright Row** | Not specified | **Lightest by explicit instruction** — "the lighter bands are preferred," for a population with compromised shoulders **[SOURCED]**. |

shows band colour alone will mislead users comparing a bicep curl to a deadlift.

## Start / resting tension classification

⚠️ **[DERIVED]** — No Jaquish source classifies movements this way. This is my analysis from three
sourced variables: **(1)** where the band is anchored (ground plate / your own shoulders / your foot),
**(2)** how far the bar sits from that anchor at the start position, and **(3)** whether the band is
doubled or singled. It is internally consistent and mechanically sound, but it is not a published
X3 concept — do not attribute it to Jaquish.

### HIGH start tension
| Movement | Why |
|---|---|
| **Overhead Press** | Floor-anchored, bar starts at **chin level** — the greatest anchor-to-bar distance of any start position. You must squat down, lift the bar under load, and stand up into an already-tensioned hold. The kneeling regression and the Ultra Light band's overhead pitch both exist to mitigate this. The brief's example is correct. |

### MEDIUM–HIGH start tension
| Movement | Why |
|---|---|
| **Calf Raise** | Doubled band, floor-anchored, bar held at **full arm's length standing upright** — nearly the entire tension is present before you move, because the heel travel adds only a few inches. Highest start-tension-to-working-tension ratio in the library. |
| **Chest Press** | Self-anchored around the back and **pre-tensioned by the wrap itself**; the setup is documented as feeling snug/tight just to get into. Bar starts at the chest, so at the routing's minimum stretch. |
| **Tricep Press** | Same self-anchored wrap (slightly lower on the shoulders). Pre-tensioned at setup; bar starts high at the forehead, near the shoulder anchor. |

### MEDIUM start tension
| Movement | Why |
|---|---|
| **Front Squat** | Floor-anchored, bar at shoulder height at the start. Substantial stretch standing still, but the start *is* the top of the movement, and load is capped by shoulder tolerance. |
| **Split Squat** | Same geometry; higher absolute tension when using the Squat Belt with doubled bands. |
| **Pec Crossover** | Self-anchored across the back (so never slack), but arms start near the body and only one crosses at a time — lower peak than the chest press. |

### LOW–MEDIUM start tension
| Movement | Why |
|---|---|
| **Bent Row** | Bent-over, arms hanging, bar low and near the plate anchor. A little more than the deadlift because of the forward-pitched torso. |
| **Upright Row** | Floor-anchored but the bar **starts down at arms' length**, near the anchor — the inverse of the overhead press despite the identical setup. Only the slight elbow bend holds tension. Part of why it is the accessible substitution. |

### LOW start tension
| Movement | Why |
|---|---|
| **Deadlift** | Bar starts **just below the knee**, closest to the floor anchor. Jaquish explicitly says keep only "a little bit of tension" at the bottom and reverse before losing it. Genuinely the nearest-to-slack start — the brief's intuition is right. |
| **Bicep Curl** | **Singled band** (≈half force at equal stretch) plus a low, arms-down start. Low for a routing reason rather than a geometry reason. |

### Design implication
Two independent axes must not be conflated:
- **Start tension** = how loaded you are before rep one. Drives *safety*, setup difficulty, and the
  risk of the "never step off the plate under tension" warning.
- **Strong range** = where in the rep you can produce most force. Drives *programming* and rep quality.

The **overhead press is the extreme case**: HIGH start tension at the **weak** range (chin level),
climbing to peak tension at the **strong** range (overhead). The **deadlift is the opposite**: LOW
start tension at the weak range, climbing to peak at the strong range. Both are "correct" X3
loading; they just feel completely different, and an app that treats start tension as a proxy for
difficulty will mis-rank them.

2. **Doubled vs singled is undocumented for five movements**: Overhead Press, Front Squat, Split
   Squat, Pec Crossover, Upright Row. Sources say only "wrapped under the ground plate."
3. **Bent Row grip is genuinely ambiguous.** The page says "supinated" but glosses it as "pointing
   outward," and uses identical wording for the bicep curl's grip. Verify against the official video.
4. **Pull-day exercise order differs between sources** (Workout Card layout vs site navigation).
   I recommend site-nav order: Deadlift → Bent Row → Bicep Curl → Calf Raise.
5. **Ultra Light band has no published lb range** — only "less than White at equivalent stretch."
6. **Elite band's 110–600+ lb** does not state whether that is singled or doubled.
7. **X3 Performance / ParaForce band forces are not published** at all.
8. **No official per-exercise band chart exists.** My load ranking is derived. The company's actual
   position is per-exercise individual progression from the lightest band.
9. **Start/resting tension is my framework, not X3's.** Mechanically grounded but unpublished.
10. **Reddit r/X3Bar was unreachable** from this environment (all reddit.com and old.reddit.com
    access blocked), so no community consensus data on real-world band-per-exercise choices is
    included. This is the biggest remaining gap for calibrating default band suggestions.
11. **Hypertrophy Program movements are named but not documented** — Band Laterals, Overhead Tricep
    Extension / Skull Crusher, Seated Calf Raise, Shallow Front Squat, Stiff Leg Deadlift, Seated
    Wrist Curl have no public form pages. Nothing here about their setup, ranges, or loading.
12. **Strong range is only explicitly stated for four movements** (Chest Press, Overhead Press, Pec
    Crossover, Bent Row, plus Bicep Curl fairly clearly). The rest are marked [DERIVED].
13. **The current official exercise pages are behind a customer login.** Everything per-exercise here
    comes from Internet Archive snapshots captured June–October 2025. Content may have changed since.
14. **Jaquish's superiority claims are contested.** BarBend's review states the scientific evidence
    does not support X3 being better than free weights, and that research shows variable resistance
    is comparably effective rather than superior. The 7x differential, the 557 lb peak force, and the
    growth-hormone/fat-loss claims are company claims. Present them as X3's position, not as fact.
