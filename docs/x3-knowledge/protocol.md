# The X3 Training Protocol — Reference

**Subject:** The X3 Bar variable-resistance system and the training protocol prescribed by Dr. John Jaquish (Jaquish Biomedical), as described in *Weight Lifting Is a Waste of Time (So Is Cardio, and There's a Better Way to Have the Body You Want)* (Jaquish & Alkire, 2020) and in official Jaquish Biomedical support documentation.

**Compiled:** 2026-09-07 from public sources only.
**Status:** Research reference for product development. Not medical advice.

## 1. What the system physically is

- **X3 Bar** — a short, thick steel bar, **21.5 in long, 1.25 in diameter, ~4 lb**, with revolving hook assemblies at each end that the bands clip into. The revolving hooks exist to stop wrist torsion when the band twists. **[OFFICIAL]**
- **Ground plate** — a marine-grade HDPE plate, **10 in × 19 in × 1 in, ~7 lb**, with a channel that seats the band under the midfoot. Its stated purpose is to prevent foot pronation and the discomfort of standing on a bare band. **[OFFICIAL]**
- **Bands** — 41-inch looped latex bands in a custom multi-layer construction. **[OFFICIAL]**

## 2. Variable resistance theory — why bands, and why the strong range

### 2.1 The core argument

Every source, official and critical, agrees on the *shape* of the argument, so state it cleanly:

1. Human force capacity is **not constant across a joint's range of motion**. You are weak at the bottom of a bench press (chest, elbows flexed, poor leverage) and strong at the top (near lockout, good leverage).
2. With a **constant load** — a barbell, a dumbbell, a plate stack — the weight you can use is **capped by your weakest position**. The bar has to move through the sticking point, so the sticking point sets the ceiling.
3. Therefore, for the rest of the range, the load is **submaximal relative to what the muscle could handle there**. The strong range is, in Jaquish's framing, chronically under-stimulated.
4. An elastic band's tension **rises as it stretches**, so resistance climbs through the range and peaks where you are strongest. The load curve is matched to the strength curve.

**[OFFICIAL]** This is the "force variance" principle, one of three named X3 fundamentals (the other two are Constant Tension and Diminishing Range).

### 2.2 The "7x" claim

Jaquish's headline number is that humans are approximately **seven times stronger in the strong ("impact-ready") range than in the weak range**. He sources this to his own 2015 research protocol, describing forces in impact-ready ranges as, in the company's words, "seven times greater" than figures in the American College of Sports Medicine database. **[OFFICIAL]**

Practical numbers he gives for his own chest press, which vary between interviews:

- ~**100 lb** at the bottom (weakest, band least stretched)
- ~**300 lb** at mid-range
- ~**550 lb** at the top (strongest, band most stretched)

Other retellings give 500 or 540 at the top. **[CONTESTED]** — the top figure is not stable across his own interviews.

He also describes the strength curve as an **S-curve**: rising from weak to strong, then dipping slightly at true lockout as the nervous system reflexively disengages to protect the joint. **[OFFICIAL]**

### 2.3 Bands vs. chains

Company position: **chains add resistance linearly** (each link that lifts off the floor adds a fixed increment), whereas **bands add resistance exponentially**, which better matches the strength curve. **[OFFICIAL]**

### 2.4 The joint-load argument

Because resistance is lowest where the joint is most vulnerable (deep flexion, stretched position) and highest where it is most stable, the company argues X3 produces less cumulative joint damage than constant load. **[OFFICIAL]** Independent reviewers broadly grant this one — reviewers note it is genuinely easier on joints, and one first-person 12-week reviewer reported being able to squat and deadlift to failure for the first time in his life without the joint pain he got from barbells. **[EVIDENCE, anecdotal]**

### 2.5 What the literature actually shows — the honest version

**[EVIDENCE]** Variable resistance training (VRT) is a real, studied modality. The evidence supports "comparable, sometimes modestly better for strength/power," **not** "categorically superior for hypertrophy."

- **Soria-Gila et al. (2015), "Effects of Variable Resistance Training on Maximal Strength: A Meta-Analysis," *J Strength Cond Res* 29(11):3260–3270.** Found VRT produced significantly greater mean strength gains (~5.03 kg) than conventional training. **This paper was RETRACTED** in *JSCR* 2018;32(11):e56, reportedly for statistical errors that changed the conclusion. This is important because it is one of the load-bearing citations in the VRT-is-better argument. **[EVIDENCE — retraction confirmed via multiple secondary sources; I was unable to fetch the retraction notice itself, which is paywalled, so treat the exact reason ("statistical errors") as [UNVERIFIED] even though the retraction itself is well attested.]**
- **Nilo dos Santos et al. (2018)** subsequently found **no statistically significant difference** between VRT and conventional resistance training. **[EVIDENCE]**
- A **2023 systematic review** in *Sport Sciences for Health* examined VRT vs. conventional RT specifically for **hypertrophy**. **[UNVERIFIED — I located the citation but did not read the full text; do not quote its conclusion.]**
- Garage Gym Reviews cites *JSCR* work finding **no significant difference in muscle recruitment** between band and traditional resistance training. **[EVIDENCE, secondhand]**

**Bottom line for a product:** the variable-resistance premise is defensible as "a legitimate training modality with a coherent mechanical rationale." It is not defensible as "3x better than weights." See §9.

## 3. The single-set-to-failure prescription

### 3.1 Is it really one set? Yes.

**[OFFICIAL]** The X3 12-Week Program prescribes **exactly one working set per exercise**, taken to complete muscular failure. The support site has a dedicated article titled "Why only one set? How long should I rest between exercises?" and another titled "How many sets do I need to do with the X3?"

The company's stated rationale, in substance: variable resistance lets you fatigue the muscle across *all* ranges of motion within a single set, so the usual reason for doing multiple sets — that one set only exhausts part of your capacity — no longer applies. Jaquish's phrasing on the support site is that the appeal of variable resistance is being able to fatigue the muscle in all ranges of motion in one set.

Jaquish goes further in interviews: he claims **multiple sets actively "set you back"** by adding damage without adding stimulus, and he uses a suntan analogy — one sufficient exposure produces the adaptation; repeating it doesn't multiply the result. **[OFFICIAL]**

### 3.2 The one significant exception

**[OFFICIAL]** The separate **X3 Hypertrophy Program** — co-developed with strength coach **Matt Wenning** — is explicitly higher volume and **uses supersets**, with sessions "typically under an hour." So the company itself ships a program that abandons the one-set rule when the goal is size rather than strength-per-minute.

This matters for a product: **X3 has two official programs with materially different set structures.** Do not model "X3" as a single prescription.

| | 12-Week Program | Hypertrophy Program |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Strong and lean, minimum time | Maximize muscle size |
| Sets | One per exercise | Supersets, higher volume |
| Session length | 10–20 min | Under an hour |
| Rest | "Long enough to catch your breath" | 20 s within a superset; 2 min between superset rounds **[OFFICIAL]** |
| Structure | Push/Pull split | Agonist/antagonist pairing |

### 3.3 What the evidence says about training to failure

**[EVIDENCE]** This is one of the clearest places where the protocol runs ahead of the literature.

- **Refalo et al. (2023), "Influence of Resistance Training Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis"** (PMID 36334240) analyzed ~15 studies. Conclusion: **there is no evidence that training to momentary muscular failure is superior to non-failure training for hypertrophy.** There is a *small* advantage to training closer to failure (effect sizes roughly 0.15–0.21), which is a trivial-to-small effect.
- Practical read: stopping **1–3 reps short of failure** produces essentially equivalent growth with substantially less fatigue. Failure roughly doubles acute neuromuscular fatigue cost.
- Outlift's review additionally cites work suggesting **intermediate lifters build more muscle stopping shy of failure**, and notes that training *past* failure (which is what X3 prescribes) has no supporting evidence for enhanced hypertrophy.

**[INFERRED]** The X3 one-set-to-failure design is best understood as an **efficiency** argument (maximum stimulus per minute of training) rather than a **maximum-hypertrophy** argument. That framing is honest and defensible. "One set to failure beats multiple sets" is not.

## 4. The rep model — exactly how a set ends

This is the most distinctive part of the protocol and the part a training app most needs to get right.

### 4.1 The three phases

**[OFFICIAL]** A single X3 set has three phases, run continuously with **no rest between them**:

**Phase 1 — Full-range reps.**
Perform complete repetitions through the full working range, with constant tension. Continue until you **can no longer reach the top (strongest) position**. You are not stopping because you're tired in general; you are stopping because a *specific range* has failed.

**Phase 2 — Diminishing range / mid-range partials.**
Without racking, resting, or releasing tension, immediately continue doing reps through **whatever range you can still complete**. Because band tension is highest at the top, the top is what fails first. So the movement naturally shortens: you now work the middle of the range. The company's guidance indicates roughly **five or more additional reps** here. **[OFFICIAL, approximate]**

**Phase 3 — Bottom-range partials to true failure.**
The range keeps collapsing. You end working only the bottom few inches of the movement. Company guidance describes roughly **2–3 final minimal-range reps**. **[OFFICIAL, approximate]**

### 4.2 The termination condition

**[OFFICIAL]** The set ends when **you cannot move the bar even one inch.** This is stated in exactly those terms in the official "How do I use the X3 Bar Elite?" article. Jaquish's own phrasing in interviews is that the last repetition might only be one inch.

Practitioner reports converge: users describe calling the set when travel has collapsed to roughly 5–10% of full range, and describe the whole set taking **roughly 30–90 seconds**, commonly ~20–25 full reps before the partials begin. **[EVIDENCE, community-sourced — not official]**

### 4.3 The direction the partials move — read this carefully

**[INFERRED, high confidence]** Jaquish states the order plainly: you fatigue "the stronger part of the movement, then the middle part of the movement, and then the bottom part."

For a press, the **top** is the shortened/contracted muscle position and the **most stretched band position** (highest tension). The **bottom** is the lengthened/stretched muscle position and the **least stretched band position** (lowest tension).

Therefore **X3's diminishing-range partials migrate toward the lengthened position** — they are not "shortened partials" in the sense that term is used in the hypertrophy literature. A product should not describe them as shortened partials.

**[CONTESTED]** The real critique is subtler and is covered in §9.3: the lengthened position is where band tension is *lowest*, so the position that current evidence suggests drives disproportionate hypertrophy is the least-loaded part of every X3 rep.

**[UNVERIFIED]** I could not find an official X3 statement addressing lengthened-position hypertrophy research directly. Some community descriptions of the partials are ambiguous about direction. If precision matters for your app's coaching copy, this is worth confirming against the paid video library.

### 4.4 The two non-negotiable technique rules

**[OFFICIAL]** Both appear in the official usage instructions:

1. **Constant tension — never let the band go slack at the bottom.** The muscle stays loaded for the entire set. This is a named X3 fundamental. It also produces a deliberate hypoxia/occlusion-like effect the company credits for the extreme pump.
2. **Never lock out the joints at the top.** Stop just short of full extension. Locking out transfers load to the skeleton and breaks constant tension.

**[INFERRED]** These two rules together mean the "full range" in Phase 1 is already a *slightly* truncated range at both ends — no slack at the bottom, no lockout at the top.

### 4.5 The physiological story the company tells

**[OFFICIAL]** The company frames the two phases as hitting two different mechanisms:

- **Strong-range fatigue (Phase 1)** → *myofibrillar* effect. Structural fatigue while ATP stores are still largely intact.
- **Diminishing-range fatigue (Phases 2–3)** → *sarcoplasmic* effect. Energy stores deplete, producing deeper fatigue and a large blood-flow response.

**[CONTESTED]** The clean myofibrillar/sarcoplasmic split is a contested model in exercise science generally; it is a useful mental model, not settled physiology. Do not present it as established fact.

## 5. The 15–40 rep window and band selection

This is the progression engine of the whole program, and it is unusually well specified.

### 5.1 The rule

**[OFFICIAL]**

- **Minimum 15 reps.** If you cannot complete at least 15 slow, controlled reps, **the band is too heavy — drop to a lighter band.**
- **Maximum 40 reps.** When you can complete **40 slow and controlled reps** with good form, **move up to the next band.**
- Start every exercise with the **lightest band** and work up.
- For the Elite band specifically: you should be able to do **40 slow controlled reps with the black band** before moving to Elite.

### 5.2 The rep minimum is framed as a safety rule, not just a programming rule

**[OFFICIAL]** This is an important nuance. The official answer to "how do I pick the right band" frames sub-15-rep loading as *dangerous*: the company's position is that if you can't do 15 reps you're under a load you have no business under, you could lose control of the bar, and the band can snap back at you. This is not a hypertrophy argument — it is a "you cannot safely fail under this load" argument.

### 5.3 Why the reps are high

**[OFFICIAL]** Jaquish's stated reasoning: because tension climbs through the range, total work per rep is high even though the *starting* load is light. Rep count is therefore not comparable to barbell rep count. He also warns explicitly against going so heavy that you can only manage ~3 reps, because that defeats the point of variable resistance — you'd never reach the range where the band is loading you properly.

### 5.4 Progression in practice

**[EVIDENCE, first-person]** A documented 12-week run starting on the lightest (white) band progressed like this:

| Exercise | First upgrade | Second upgrade |
|---|---|---|
| Chest press | Light gray (wk 2) | Dark gray (wk 7) |
| Calf raise | Light gray (wk 2) | Dark gray (wk 4) |
| Tricep press | Light gray (wk 3) | Dark gray (wk 9) |
| Deadlift | Light gray (wk 9) | — |

The reviewer never reached the black band in 12 weeks. **Useful calibration for a product: band progression is per-exercise, not global, and it is slow.** Expect users to sit on the same band for many weeks on some lifts.

**[INFERRED]** A tracking app therefore needs **per-exercise band state**, not a single "current band" per user.

## 6. Tempo

**[OFFICIAL]** **2–3 seconds up, 2–3 seconds down.** The company's phrasing is that this rep speed produces the best results. Jaquish sometimes says "two seconds up, two seconds down" in interviews.

Everything is slow and controlled. No momentum at the start of the rep, no bouncing out of the bottom, no lockout, no relaxation point.

### An important correction

**[UNVERIFIED / likely false]** The X3 protocol as published does **not** prescribe eccentric emphasis — that is, it does not ask for a longer lowering phase than lifting phase. Every official source I found specifies a **symmetric** 2–3 s concentric / 2–3 s eccentric. I found no official X3 instruction for a 4-second, 5-second, or otherwise extended eccentric.

If your product currently assumes eccentric emphasis in the X3 protocol, **that assumption is not sourced** and should be corrected or clearly labeled as an app-specific addition. (Eccentric emphasis is a legitimate general training technique — it just isn't what X3 prescribes.)

### Implied set duration

**[INFERRED]** At 4–6 s per rep and 15–40 reps, Phase 1 alone runs **60 s to 240 s** of continuous tension. Community reports of "30–90 second" sets suggest most people run nearer the low end (~15–20 full reps) rather than the 40-rep ceiling. There is tension between the official 40-rep ceiling and the reported set durations. **[CONTESTED]**

## 7. Rest

### 7.1 Between exercises

**[OFFICIAL]** "Rest between sets only long enough to catch your breath." That is the entire official prescription — it is **breath-limited, not clock-limited.**

**[EVIDENCE, first-person]** In practice, a documented 12-week user settled on **30–120 seconds** between movements. Whole four-exercise workouts were timed at **6:42 to 8:11**, including setup and reading instructions.

**[OFFICIAL]** For the **Hypertrophy Program** only: **20 seconds** between the paired exercises inside a superset, **2 minutes** between superset rounds.

### 7.2 Between workouts

**[OFFICIAL]** The recovery model is the justification for the very high frequency:

- Jaquish cites muscle biopsy work indicating **you are recovered after ~36 hours**, and elsewhere that **muscle protein synthesis concludes in ~36 hours**. The support site also references a recovery window for muscular tissue peaking around **24 hours** post-exercise. **[CONTESTED — the company's own materials give both 24 h and 36 h in different places.]**
- Because Push and Pull days work **non-overlapping muscle groups**, alternating them gives each group roughly **a day and a half** before it is trained again.
- Target frequency: **each muscle group ~3× per week.**

**[OFFICIAL]** Jaquish claims X3 produces **little to no DOMS** — his position is that you're building muscle without getting sore, and that a few minutes after training you are functionally fine. He attributes this to the load profile sparing the lengthened position, where most eccentric damage occurs.

**[INFERRED]** That last mechanism is worth noting because it cuts both ways: the same feature that reduces soreness (low tension in the stretched position) is exactly what critics identify as the protocol's hypertrophy weakness. See §9.3.

### 7.3 Where real users diverge

**[EVIDENCE]** Two independent long-form first-person accounts both **reduced frequency below the prescription**:

- One dropped from the prescribed 4×/week to **2×/week** (one Push, one Pull) after three weeks, citing weight gain and declining performance.
- A StrongFirst forum practitioner concluded the program demanded excessive recovery capacity and moved to a **2-session/week** Monday/Thursday split, describing the prescribed volume as "too much, instead of 'not enough.'"

**[INFERRED]** This is a real, repeated signal. A product built on this protocol should probably support **user-adjusted frequency** rather than hard-coding 6 days/week, and should watch for performance regression as a deload trigger.

## 8. The 12-week program structure

### 8.1 Schedule

**[OFFICIAL]**

**Weeks 1–4 — Foundation phase. 4 training days, 3 rest days.**

| Day | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Push | Pull | Rest | Push | Pull | Rest | Rest |

Stated purpose: let the body acclimate to a stimulus it has not experienced — taking muscles to complete fatigue, which the company argues is effectively impossible with constant-load weights.

**Weeks 5–12 — Strength & muscle phase. 6 training days, 1 rest day.**

| Day | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Push | Pull | Push | Pull | Push | Pull | Rest |

**Session length: 10–20 minutes.** **[OFFICIAL]**

### 8.2 The eight core movements

**[OFFICIAL]**

| Push Day | Pull Day |
|---|---|
| Chest Press | Deadlift |
| Tricep Press | Bent Row |
| Overhead Press | Bicep Curl |
| Front Squat | Calf Raise |

Push days cover **chest, triceps, quads, deltoids**. Pull days cover **traps, lats, glutes, hamstrings, biceps, calves**. **[OFFICIAL]**

**[INFERRED]** Note the order matters and is a known weak point: on Push day, **Chest Press and Overhead Press both heavily tax the anterior delts and triceps**, so whichever comes second is pre-fatigued. A first-person reviewer measured this as roughly halving reps on the second pressing movement and explicitly flagged it as a design flaw. **[EVIDENCE, first-person]**

### 8.3 Progressions within the 12 weeks

**[OFFICIAL]**

- **After week 5:** **Pec Crossover** and **Split Squat** variations are introduced.
- **After week 8:** advanced techniques — **breath control** and **stabilization firing**.

**[UNVERIFIED]** The exact week-by-week substitution schedule (which day, replacing what) is inside the paid program. I could not verify it. Do not fabricate a week-by-week table.

### 8.4 Deloads

**[UNVERIFIED]** I found **no evidence of a programmed deload week** anywhere in the 12-week structure. The only scheduled recovery is the weekly rest day (and the three rest days per week during weeks 1–4). If your product wants to offer deloads, present them as an app feature, not as part of the X3 protocol.

### 8.5 The full exercise library

**[OFFICIAL]** Beyond the core eight:

- **Deltoids:** Overhead Press, Upright Row *(the upright row is the officially sanctioned substitute for people who can't do overhead press due to shoulder issues)*
- **Pectorals:** Chest Press, Pec Crossover
- **Triceps:** Tricep Press
- **Biceps:** Bicep Curl
- **Lats:** Bent Row
- **Quads / glutes / abs:** Front Squat, Split Squat
- **Calves / hamstrings:** Calf Raise
- **Traps / hamstrings:** Deadlift

**Hypertrophy Program additions [OFFICIAL]:** Band Laterals, Overhead Tricep Extension, Seated Calf Raise, Shallow Front Squat, Stiff Leg Deadlift, Seated Wrist Curl.

**[INFERRED]** Notable absences: there is **no vertical pull** (no pull-up/pulldown pattern) and **no direct hamstring curl** in the core eight. The lat work is entirely the bent row. This is a genuine coverage gap, and independent reviewers cite "limited exercise selection" as a real limitation.

## 9. Criticisms and controversies — the fair version

The method attracts strong reactions in both directions. Here is what actually holds up.

### 9.1 The "3x" claim

**[CONTESTED]** The product's name and marketing rest on building muscle **three times faster** than conventional weights.

- **There are no clinical trials directly comparing the X3 program to free weights or machines.** Multiple independent reviewers (BarBend, Garage Gym Reviews, Joe Cannon, Outlift) all state this independently. This is the single most consistent criticism.
- Outlift's specific charge: the study Jaquish invokes for "3x" investigated **adding bands to free weights** (accommodating resistance), which is a *different intervention* from band-only training.
- Independent reviewers converge on: variable resistance appears to be **as effective**, not more effective, than constant resistance.

### 9.2 Problems with the cited literature

**[EVIDENCE]**

1. **Retracted meta-analysis.** The Soria-Gila 2015 VRT meta-analysis, a foundational citation for VRT superiority, was retracted (*JSCR* 2018;32(11):e56). The 2018 dos Santos re-analysis found no significant difference.
2. **The elastic-band-vs-machines study cuts the wrong way.** In the study of sedentary middle-aged women often cited, the **machine group gained roughly twice the lean mass and lost roughly twice the fat** as the band group. It shows bands are *viable*, not superior.
3. **Growth hormone citation mismatch.** Joe Cannon reports that the GH meta-analysis referenced does not appear to have used elastic bands or the X3 program at all — the underlying research involved free weights, sprinting, and vibration training.
4. **Self-citation / conflict of interest.** Garage Gym Reviews notes Jaquish **co-authored one of the studies cited on his own website** to promote the product, and cautions about the inherent bias.
5. **The "200–2,600% growth hormone increase" figure** on the company site is presented without an accessible primary citation. **[UNVERIFIED]**

### 9.3 The lengthened-position problem — the strongest technical critique

**[EVIDENCE]** This is the most substantive scientific objection and the one most worth understanding.

Since roughly 2023, a converging body of work indicates that **training a muscle at long (stretched) muscle lengths drives disproportionate hypertrophy**:

- Wolf, Schoenfeld, Steele et al., systematic review in the *International Journal of Strength and Conditioning* (2023): partial and full ROM produce similar growth **on average**, but partials performed at **long muscle lengths** shift the effect in their favor (SMD ≈ −0.28 favoring lengthened partials).
- Gastrocnemius trial (*JSCR*, Sept 2023, PMID 37015016): **greater hypertrophy from partial ROM training at long muscle lengths.**
- Outlift cites a systematic review of 26 studies reporting on the order of **three times as much muscle growth** when the challenge is applied at longer muscle lengths.
- The consensus framing: full ROM is "lengthened + middle + shortened," and the **lengthened portion appears to do most of the hypertrophy work**.

**The problem for X3:** a band's tension is **lowest exactly where the muscle is longest**. So the X3 load curve is inverse to the hypertrophy-stimulus curve implied by this literature. Jaquish argues the strong range is under-loaded by barbells; critics argue the *stretched* range is under-loaded by bands — and that the stretched range is the one that matters more for growth.

**[INFERRED]** Both things can be true. The mechanical argument for loading the strong range is coherent; it just may not be the range that matters most for hypertrophy specifically. This is a genuine, live scientific disagreement, not a settled debunking.

### 9.4 Training past failure

**[EVIDENCE]** As covered in §3.3 — no evidence supports past-failure work as superior for hypertrophy, and the failure-vs-non-failure literature (Refalo 2023) finds failure itself is not required. X3's core intensity prescription is stronger than the evidence demands.

### 9.5 Credentials

**[CONTESTED]** Jaquish's PhD is from **Rushmore University**, which was **unaccredited** at the time. This is the most common ad hominem leveled at him, and he has addressed it publicly at length.

His stated position: he chose Rushmore because it let him study his own bone-density technology, which conventional universities would not permit; Rushmore was founded in 1992 and he says it has since pursued/obtained accreditation (he references the UK-based Charter Management Institute); he argues accreditation criteria are partly anachronistic (physical library volumes); and he says he plans to publish his dissertation publicly. He also states the university offered him a full ride partly for its own marketing benefit. **[OFFICIAL — his own account]**

**[UNVERIFIED]** I could not independently verify Rushmore University's current accreditation status, nor confirm the CMI relationship. Report the *dispute*, not a verdict.

He has also publicly addressed accusations of **photo retouching** (his position: standard industry practice, and video cannot be retouched) and openly discusses being on **testosterone replacement therapy** at a low daily dose. **[OFFICIAL — his own account]** The TRT disclosure is relevant context for physique-based marketing claims.

### 9.6 Bone density background

**[EVIDENCE]** Jaquish's prior invention is the **bioDensity** device (he was CTO of Performance Health Systems), which underpins the **OsteoStrong** franchise. He co-authored *Osteogenic Loading: A New Modality to Facilitate Bone Density Development* (2012).

- A 2013 study reports bone density improvement of **7.34%** across spine and hip over one year. **[UNVERIFIED — I did not read the primary paper.]**
- Osteogenic loading research suggests roughly **3–9× body weight loading** is needed to drive BMD change.
- **[EVIDENCE]** A critical appraisal notes OsteoStrong research is "mainly limited to small observational studies at risk of bias" due to conflict of interest, imprecision, publication in a predatory journal, participants concurrently on anti-resorptive medications, or poor reporting quality — with only **three peer-reviewed studies** published on OsteoStrong efficacy.

**Important:** the bone-density work is a **separate device and modality** from X3. Do not let X3 marketing borrow bone-density claims. The company's own support site has an article on whether X3 helps osteoporosis, but the osteogenic loading data are from bioDensity, not X3. **[INFERRED]**

### 9.7 The anti-cardio position

**[OFFICIAL]** Jaquish argues cardio is counterproductive: it **up-regulates cortisol**, and he claims weight lost through cardio is disproportionately muscle. The support site has an article titled "Why do you recommend against cardio with the X3?"

**[CONTESTED]** This is where even sympathetic reviewers push back. A favorable book review (rating it 7/10, reviewer went on to buy the system) still identified **"downplaying the health benefits of cardio-intensive exercise"** as the book's clearest weakness, and acknowledged reasonable criticisms of cherry-picked evidence.

**[EVIDENCE]** The cardiorespiratory fitness literature (VO2max as an all-cause-mortality predictor) is among the most robust in exercise epidemiology. **[UNVERIFIED — I did not fetch specific VO2max/mortality citations in this pass; if the product makes health claims, source these properly before shipping.]**

### 9.8 Practical limitations reviewers cite

**[EVIDENCE]**

- **Price** — around $550 for the system; one practitioner called it "absurd."
- **Limited exercise selection** compared to a full home gym; limited progression without buying more bands.
- **Time efficiency of 40-rep sets** — BarBend notes high rep counts are actually *less* time-efficient than heavy weights for reaching fatigue, which cuts against the core efficiency pitch at the top end of the rep window.
- **Range-of-motion fit** — negative reviews from smaller users / those with shorter arms, who struggle with ROM on a fixed 21.5 in bar.
- **Band wear** — some customers report visible wear within weeks; comments on Joe Cannon's review mention fraying. The company's position is that surface wear on outer layers is cosmetic.

## 10. Warmup, stretching, and "greasing the groove"

### 10.1 Warmup

**[OFFICIAL]** **No dedicated warmup is prescribed.** The official answer to "Do I need to warm up?" is that it is not necessary, and that **the first few repetitions serve as the warmup.**

**[INFERRED]** This is mechanically coherent within the system: because band tension is near-zero at the start of the range and the first reps of a 15–40 rep set are far from failure, the early reps are inherently light. It is still a notably aggressive stance relative to mainstream practice, especially for the deadlift and front squat. A product may reasonably offer an optional warmup without contradicting the protocol.

### 10.2 "Greasing the groove" — this is NOT an X3 term

**[UNVERIFIED / misattribution warning]** **"Greasing the groove" is not Jaquish terminology.** It is a term from **Pavel Tsatsouline / StrongFirst**, describing frequent, sub-maximal, never-to-failure practice of a movement to build skill and neural efficiency.

It is close to the **philosophical opposite** of the X3 prescription, which is maximal, always-to-failure, low-frequency-per-set. Search results conflate the two only because both happen to skip a formal warmup.

**Do not attribute "greasing the groove" to X3 or Jaquish in any product copy.** If the concept is wanted, it belongs as an explicitly separate, non-X3 training mode.

### 10.3 The post-workout stretch (the "Hyperplasia Protocol")

**[OFFICIAL]** This is the one thing X3 does prescribe around the session, and it is a real named protocol. The support site has an article titled "What's the X3 Hyperplasia Protocol?"

The prescription:

- **Stretch each target muscle for ~30 seconds.**
- Do it **within about 6 minutes of finishing** the workout.
- Rationale: the constant-tension/hypoxia technique produces an unusually large blood-flow response ("pump") that can persist ~30 minutes; **stretching the fascia while the muscle is engorged** amplifies blood flow into the muscle and, in the company's model, promotes **hyperplasia** (new fiber formation) as distinct from hypertrophy (enlargement of existing fibers).

**[CONTESTED]** Hyperplasia in adult human skeletal muscle in response to training is **not established**. It is demonstrated in some animal models (notably avian stretch-overload studies); evidence in humans is weak and contested. The fascia-stretching mechanism is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**[OFFICIAL]** The protocol also has a commercial nutrition component: consuming **FortagenHP ~15 minutes before the workout** (an EAA + glucose formulation, marketed as enhancing cellular hydration and replenishing glycogen). **Flag the conflict of interest — this is a company supplement inside a company protocol.**

## 11. Nutrition and recovery guidance

**[OFFICIAL]** Brief, since the ask was brief. This accompanies the program but is separable from it.

### Protein
- Support site: **1 gram per pound of body weight** (≈ **2.2 g/kg**), based on **total** body weight, not lean mass.
- The book states **2.2–2.5 g/kg**, prioritizing **animal sources** for amino acid profile and absorption.
- Jaquish personally reports ~**220 g protein/day**.

### Fasting
- Advocates intermittent fasting, working toward **OMAD (one meal a day)**.
- On rest days he describes doing a **48-hour fasted period**.
- **Up to 50 calories** does not break the fast, per the company's stated position.
- Cites fasting producing **4–5 fold higher GH levels**. **[UNVERIFIED — no accessible primary citation.]**
- His own meal: typically carnivore-leaning; he has described "three steaks."

### Carbohydrates
- Position: **carbs are not required to build muscle.**
- With OMAD, some carbohydrate in the single meal is considered acceptable since ketosis resumes ~12 h later.

## 12. Safety — what can go wrong

### 12.1 Official X3 safety rules

**[OFFICIAL]** From the company's dedicated safety article and usage instructions. The company's own framing is that failure to follow these can result in serious injury.

1. **Maintain a firm grip on the bar at all times.**
2. **Never step off the ground plate while there is tension in the band.**
3. **Never let go of the bar while there is tension in the band.**
4. **Verify the band is fully seated in the hooks before loading.**
5. **Keep wrists straight and wrap thumbs around the bar** — except during squats.
6. **Center your weight over the middle of the band channel** on the ground plate.
7. **Band placement:** midfoot for most exercises; **ball of the foot for calf raises.**
8. **Never use X3 on slippery, unstable, or rough surfaces.**
9. **Never use X3 bands without the X3 bar and ground plate** — the company states this is unsafe due to the loads involved. (Standard bands must not be stacked; only X3 Performance Bands are designed for stacking.)
10. **Inspect bands before AND after every workout** for cracks, discoloration, punctures, tears, or other wear.
11. **Consult a physician before beginning.**
12. **If you can't do 15 reps, the band is too heavy** — framed as a safety rule (§5.2).

### 12.2 Band failure — the real risk

**[EVIDENCE]** This is the failure mode a product should take most seriously, because a band under 300–600 lb of tension stores serious elastic energy.

- **Documented X3 band snap:** at least one reviewer reports having a band snap during use.
- **General resistance-band injury literature:** ophthalmologists report an **increase in eye injuries** from resistance bands, ranging from superficial periorbital bruising to **permanent, irreversible vision loss**. The mechanism is loss of grip on a band under tension, causing snap-back.
- A documented case at a fitness facility: a broken band snapped back into a user's face, requiring stitches and narrowly missing the eye.
- General guidance from band manufacturers and safety sources:
  - Inspect **before every use** for cuts, nicks, scratches, cracks, punctures, discoloration, thin spots.
  - **Never release a band under tension.**
  - **Replace, don't repair.** Replace bands used above ~40–50 lb tension at the *first* sign of damage.
  - Consider **protective eyewear**, particularly for overhead movements.

**[INFERRED]** Given X3 operates at 100–600 lb — far above the 40–50 lb threshold at which general guidance says replace-at-first-sign — the inspection discipline should be treated as **mandatory, not advisory**. A training app tracking X3 should arguably prompt a band inspection check as part of session start, and track band age against the ~7-year (or 1-year warranty) horizon.

### 12.3 Band care

**[OFFICIAL]**

- Store away from **direct sunlight**, temperature extremes, humidity extremes, and heat sources.
- Clean with a **damp cloth only** — soap and cleaning products degrade latex strength and elasticity.
- Surface wear on the outer layers is described by the company as cosmetic; minor nicks and scratches are said not to indicate functional problems. **[CONTESTED — this sits uneasily against general band-safety guidance to replace at first sign of damage on high-tension bands. Where the two conflict, the conservative general guidance is the safer default for a product to surface.]**

### 12.4 Joint and movement-specific concerns

**[EVIDENCE]** Joe Cannon specifically flags **overhead press, bent-over rows, and triceps pushdowns** as the movements warranting the most caution, with proper form and correct band placement being critical.

**[OFFICIAL]** The company itself acknowledges shoulder limitations: the sanctioned substitution for people who cannot perform the overhead press is the **upright row**. **[INFERRED — note that the upright row is itself commonly flagged in the strength literature as an impingement risk for some shoulders, so this substitution is not obviously conservative.]**

### 12.5 Respiratory / cardiovascular considerations

**[OFFICIAL]** The company has a support article titled "What do I do when I run out of breath during squats before my muscles are exhausted?" — which is direct acknowledgment that **high-rep front squats to failure commonly cause breathlessness before local muscular failure.**

**[UNVERIFIED / general exercise science, not X3-specific]** Long sets under continuous tension with restricted breathing invite Valsalva-like pressure spikes. Standard contraindications for the Valsalva maneuver include unstable angina or recent MI, severe valvular disease, recent TIA or stroke, and uncontrolled hypertension. **I found no X3-specific contraindication list.** Do not present this as X3 guidance — present it as general caution and defer to a physician.

**[UNVERIFIED]** I found **no official X3 contraindication list** at all — nothing on hernia, pregnancy, uncontrolled hypertension, retinal issues, or latex allergy. **Latex allergy is an obvious concern given the bands are latex, and I found no official warning about it.** This is a real gap in the official materials and a real product-liability consideration.

### 12.6 The "past failure" risk

**[INFERRED]** The termination condition — grinding until the bar won't move an inch — combined with **no spotter, no rack, and a band under high tension anchored to your feet** is a meaningfully different failure scenario than a barbell. The mitigations are structural: the load *drops* as you descend (the band unloads), and the rep minimum ensures you're never under a load you can't control. That is a genuinely sensible safety design. But it depends entirely on rules 1–3 above being followed without exception.
